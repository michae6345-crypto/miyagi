Scope note: general US law only. Children's law (COPPA, FERPA, student privacy, kids codes, app-store age laws) belongs to another researcher; minors appear here only where a general privacy law has a minors-specific provision. ## 1. CCPA/CPRA: is a pre-revenue startup a "business", what applies if not, and the CPPA's 2025 regulations

### Takeaway
miyagi is almost certainly **not** a CCPA "business" today. It has no revenue (the threshold is $26,625,000), it does not buy, sell or "share" (that is, cross-context ad targeting) the data of 100,000+ Californians, and it earns no revenue from selling data. Even at 100,000 users, collecting data alone does not trigger the 100,000 prong unless miyagi also buys, sells or shares it. The CCPA's breach private right of action (Civ. Code 1798.150) is written against "the business", so it would not reach miyagi. The separate breach-notice law (1798.82) and CalOPPA do apply, with no size threshold.

### Cited Findings
- The CPPA raised the "business" revenue threshold in Civ. Code 1798.140(d)(1)(A) from $25,000,000 to **$26,625,000**, effective January 1, 2025. The same notice set 1798.150 statutory damages at **$107 to $799** per consumer per incident (was $100 to $750) and administrative fines and civil penalties at up to **$2,663** per violation, or **$7,988** for intentional violations or violations involving consumers under 16. Adjustments happen "every odd-numbered year" under 1798.199.95(d), so the next is due in January 2027 — [CPPA, "Updated Monetary Thresholds in CCPA" (page undated; adjustment effective 2025-01-01)](https://www.cppa.ca.gov/regulations/cpi_adjustment.html) (accessed 2026-11-21); [CPPA announcement, 2024-12-17](https://cppa.ca.gov/announcements/2024/20241217.html) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- The California AG describes coverage as "for-profit businesses that do business in California" that meet one of three tests: gross annual revenue over $25 million (the AG page still shows the unadjusted figure), "buy, sell, or share" the personal information of 100,000 or more California residents or households, or earn 50% or more of annual revenue from selling Californians' personal information. Nonprofits and government agencies are generally outside the law — [CA AG CCPA page, updated 2026-08-28](https://oag.ca.gov/privacy/ccpa) (accessed 2026-11-21). Discrepancy: the AG page shows $25M while the CPPA shows the adjusted $26,625,000. The CPPA figure is the operative adjusted number.
- The AG states that consumers can "only sue a business under the CCPA if there is a data breach". The data must have been stolen "in nonencrypted and nonredacted form", and the consumer must first give 30 days' written notice to cure. The AG and the CPPA enforce every other violation — [CA AG CCPA FAQ, updated 2026-08-28](https://oag.ca.gov/privacy/ccpa) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- Civ. Code 1798.150(a)(1) imposes liability for "the business' violation of the duty to implement and maintain reasonable security procedures". It covers nonencrypted, nonredacted personal information as defined in 1798.81.5(d)(1)(A), and also an "email address in combination with a password or security question and answer that would permit access to the account". Statutory damages are $100 to $750 (CPI-adjusted to $107 to $799) or actual damages. Post-breach security fixes do not count as a "cure" — [Civ. Code 1798.150 via california.public.law (code text, current)](https://california.public.law/codes/ca_civ_code_section_1798.150) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- The CPPA's regulations (11 CCR 7000 et seq.) are published as "Effective 1/1/2026". They define "sensitive personal information" to include: account log-in plus "password, or credentials allowing access to an account"; "the contents of a consumer's mail, email, and text messages unless the business is the intended recipient"; neural data; health information; and personal information of consumers the business actually knows are under 16, with willful disregard treated as actual knowledge (11 CCR 7001(bbb)) — [CPPA, CCPA Regulations PDF, effective 2026-01-01](https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/pdf/ccpa_statute_eff_20260101.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-21; despite the file name, the PDF is the regulations).
- **2025 regulations (ADMT, risk assessments, cybersecurity audits)** (secondary sources): California's Office of Administrative Law approved them in September 2025. Changes to the existing regulations took effect January 1, 2026. Risk-assessment compliance started January 1, 2026, with the first submissions to the CPPA due April 1, 2028 for assessments done in 2026–2027. ADMT compliance is due January 1, 2027. Cybersecurity-audit certifications phase in by revenue: April 1, 2028 for businesses over $100M, and April 1, 2030 for businesses under $50M. **Conflict:** one search summary gave the $50M–$100M tier's date as April 1, 2027, while my reading of the final rules is April 1, 2029. Check the Hunton summary or the regulation text before relying on that date — [Hunton, 2025](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-information-security-law/cppa-finalizes-ccpa-regulations-on-automated-decision-making-technology-risk-assessments-and-cybersecurity-audits) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Foley, 2025-09](https://www.foley.com/insights/publications/2025/09/updated-ccpa-regulations-approved-by-california-office-of-administrative-law/) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Manatt, 2025](https://www.manatt.com/insights/newsletters/client-alert/california-finalizes-rules-on-automated-decisionmaking-risk-assessments-cybersecurity-audits) (accessed 2026-11-21).

### Inferences
- miyagi has no revenue and does no ad targeting or data sales, so it fails all three CCPA prongs. The 100,000 prong turns on buying, selling or **sharing**. Adding ad pixels (Meta or Google Ads remarketing) to miyagi.study or the app could create "sharing" and, at scale, coverage. Paid plans alone would not trigger coverage until revenue passes $26.6M.
- Because 1798.150 is written against "the business" (a defined term carrying the thresholds), a non-covered startup is not exposed to CCPA breach statutory damages. It is still exposed to common-law negligence and UCL claims and to 1798.82 notice duties (Q5), and probably to the separate reasonable-security statute, Civ. Code 1798.81.5 (not re-verified; see Gaps).
- The regulations' definition of sensitive personal information maps directly onto what miyagi's screenshots can incidentally capture: log-in credentials, contents of email and messages the user receives, and health information. If miyagi ever becomes covered, screen capture would make the "limit use of sensitive PI" and risk-assessment rules relevant. Designing to avoid capturing that data now is cheap future-proofing.
- The ADMT rules apply only to covered businesses using ADMT for "significant decisions". Auto-grading a free practice lab is unlikely to be one unless grades feed into education, credential or employment decisions (not verified against the regulation text).

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve the primary statutory text of 1798.140(d), in particular whether a sole proprietorship or unincorporated two-person venture "operated for the profit" of its owners fits the definition. The AG summary says "for-profit businesses".
- I did not re-verify Civ. Code 1798.81.5 (the reasonable-security duty) to see whether it applies regardless of CCPA thresholds.
- I did not confirm whether the 2025 regulations' "significant decision" definition includes education opportunities.

## 2. CalOPPA (Bus. & Prof. Code 22575–22579) and Delaware's DOPPA

### Takeaway
CalOPPA applies to **any operator of a commercial website or online service** that collects personally identifiable information from California residents, with no revenue or user-count threshold. miyagi's site and app need a conspicuously posted privacy policy with six required items, including an effective date and a Do Not Track disclosure. The operator is in violation only if it fails to post a compliant policy within 30 days of being notified. Delaware's Online Privacy and Protection Act mirrors this for Delaware users.

### Cited Findings
- **Scope:** an "operator of a commercial Web site or online service" that collects personally identifiable information "through the Internet about individual consumers residing in California" must conspicuously post a privacy policy. A website posts it on the site; an online service makes it available under 22577(b)(5). The operator violates the section only if it fails to post within **30 days after being notified** of noncompliance — [Bus. & Prof. Code 22575 via california.public.law (code text, current)](https://california.public.law/codes/business_and_professions_code_section_22575) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- **Required contents (22575(b)):** (1) the categories of personally identifiable information collected and the categories of third parties it may be shared with; (2) if the operator offers one, the process for reviewing and requesting changes to that information; (3) how users are notified of material changes; (4) the **effective date**; (5) how the operator responds to browser "**do not track**" signals or similar mechanisms, where it collects information about activity over time and across third-party sites; (6) whether **other parties** may collect such cross-site information through the operator's service. Item 5 can be met with a clear link to a description of a protocol that offers consumers a choice (22575(b)(7)) — [Bus. & Prof. Code 22575](https://california.public.law/codes/business_and_professions_code_section_22575) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- **Delaware (6 Del. C. 1205C):** applies to operators of commercial websites, online services, apps and mobile apps that collect personally identifiable information from Delaware users. The policy must be "conspicuously available", must state its effective date, and must identify the categories of information collected. The operator violates the act if it fails to post within 30 days after notice. The Department of Justice Consumer Protection Unit enforces it. Delaware repealed its optional "safe harbor" privacy-policy regulation effective December 11, 2024, reasoning that it could not satisfy the newer Delaware Personal Data Privacy Act in some cases — [6 Del. C. 1205C, Justia 2025 code](https://law.justia.com/codes/delaware/title-6/chapter-12c/section-1205c/) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Delaware Register, 28 DE Reg 458, 2024-12-01](https://archive.regulations.delaware.gov/register/december2024/final/28%20DE%20Reg%20458%2012-01-24.htm) (accessed 2026-11-21).

### Inferences
- CalOPPA applies to miyagi now. Whether a free product is "commercial" is not defined in the excerpt, but a startup's product site and app are very likely commercial. The 30-day cure makes a lawsuit over a missing policy unlikely. A policy that misdescribes the data flows, however, is a deception problem under FTC Act Section 5 and the UCL (Q7).
- The policy should name the real data categories (Google account identifiers, screenshots, code, lesson context, telemetry, any API keys), the third-party categories (AI model providers such as Anthropic and Google, the auth and database host Supabase, the hosting provider Render, GitHub Pages for the site, any telemetry vendor), a Do Not Track statement, and an effective date. The app should link to it, not only the site.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve the CalOPPA penalty mechanism. Enforcement runs through the AG under the UCL, and the per-violation amount was not verified this session.
- I did not retrieve the full text of 22577 ("conspicuously post" for online services and apps).

## 3. Other state comprehensive privacy laws in force by November 2026, and provisions that apply regardless of size

### Takeaway
About 20 states have comprehensive privacy laws. Nearly all apply only above consumer-count thresholds (typically 25,000 to 100,000 state residents), which miyagi will not meet for a long time. The provisions that reach a tiny startup now are: (a) **Texas and Nebraska**, which exempt SBA small businesses but still bar them from **selling sensitive data without prior consent**; (b) **minors' provisions** in Colorado (SB 24-041, from October 1, 2025) and Montana (SB 297, from October 1, 2025), which apply regardless of the general thresholds; and (c) **Connecticut's 2025 overhaul** (SB 1295, from July 1, 2026), which reportedly dropped the volume threshold for controllers that process sensitive data. Maryland's under-18 sale and ad ban and Oregon's under-16 sale ban sit behind their laws' thresholds.

### Cited Findings
- **Count (secondary):** a March 2026 ABA update says 20 states have comprehensive privacy laws. Other sources say 19, depending on the cut-off date — [ABA, 2026 compliance checklist](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/litigation/resources/newsletters/corporate-counsel/2026-data-security-privacy-compliance-checklist-us-state-law-updates-ai-rules-coppa/) (accessed 2026-11-21); [IAPP, 2026](https://iapp.org/news/a/new-year-new-rules-us-state-privacy-requirements-coming-online-as-2026-begins) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- **New on January 1, 2026:** Indiana, Kentucky and Rhode Island. Indiana and Kentucky use the Virginia-style threshold of 100,000 consumers, or 25,000 consumers plus more than 50% of revenue from data sales. Rhode Island has no right to cure and no universal opt-out signal requirement; its thresholds were not captured (see Gaps) — [TrustArc (secondary)](https://trustarc.com/resource/new-in-2026-state-privacy-laws-in-indiana-kentucky-and-rhode-island/) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Taft (secondary)](https://www.taftlaw.com/news-events/law-bulletins/rhode-islands-new-privacy-law-an-overview-and-highlighted-differences/) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- **Texas TDPSA (in force since July 1, 2024):** the chapter applies only to a person that "is not a small business as defined by the United States Small Business Administration, except to the extent that Section 541.107 ... applies" (541.002(a)(3)). Section 541.107(a): a small business "may not engage in the sale of personal data that is sensitive data without receiving prior consent from the consumer". Violations carry the 541.155 civil penalty — [Tex. Bus. & Com. Code 541.002 (code text)](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._bus._and_com._code_section_541.002) (accessed 2026-11-21); [541.107 (code text)](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._bus._and_com._code_section_541.107) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Texas AG TDPSA page](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/consumer-protection/file-consumer-complaint/enforcing-privacy-and-data-security-laws-protect-texans/texas-data-privacy-and-security-act) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- **Nebraska Data Privacy Act (LB 1074, effective January 1, 2025):** it also excludes small businesses under the federal Small Business Act, but a small business may still be penalized "if it sells sensitive data without receiving consent". Secondary sources disagree on whether the April 2024 signing was April 12 or April 17 — [Nebraska AG "Protect the Good Life" data privacy page](https://protectthegoodlife.nebraska.gov/data-privacy-homepage/) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Hunton, 2024 (secondary)](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/nebraska-enacts-comprehensive-state-privacy-law) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- **Connecticut SB 1295 (Public Act 25-113; most changes effective July 1, 2026; impact assessments for processing on or after August 1, 2026)** (secondary sources): it lowers the general threshold from 100,000 to **35,000** consumers. It "removes the processing thresholds" for controllers that process consumers' sensitive data or sell personal data. It adds categorical limits on targeted advertising to minors and on selling minors' data for certain online services. It also bars design features meant to "significantly increase, sustain, or extend" a minor's use, regardless of consent — [Foley, 2026-07](https://www.foley.com/insights/publications/2026/07/connecticut-dramatically-expands-its-data-privacy-act-what-businesses-need-to-know-now/) (accessed 2026-11-21); [FPF (secondary)](https://fpf.org/blog/the-connecticut-data-privacy-act-gets-an-overhaul-again/) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Snell & Wilmer (secondary)](https://www.swlaw.com/publication/connecticut-data-privacy-act-2026-amendments/) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- **Colorado SB 24-041 (effective October 1, 2025):** the signed text says the new minors' sections "APPLY TO A CONTROLLER THAT CONDUCTS BUSINESS IN COLORADO" or targets Colorado residents, without the Colorado Privacy Act's volume thresholds. Lowenstein: it "applies regardless of revenue or data processing thresholds". A controller offering an online service to a consumer it actually knows, or willfully disregards, is a minor (under 18) owes a duty of reasonable care to avoid a heightened risk of harm to minors. The AG and district attorneys enforce — [SB 24-041 signed text, Colorado General Assembly](https://leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/2024a_041_signed.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Lowenstein, 2025-09-30 (secondary)](https://www.lowenstein.com/news-insights/publications/client-alerts/colorado-tightens-rules-on-minors-online-data-are-you-ready-for-october-1-data-privacy) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Thompson Hine (secondary)](https://www.thompsonhine.com/insights/colorado-adds-protections-for-minors-to-privacy-law/) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- **Maryland MODPA (effective October 1, 2025):** it applies to controllers handling the data of **35,000** Maryland consumers, or **10,000** consumers plus more than 20% of revenue from data sales. Controllers may not sell personal data or use it for targeted ads if they "knew or should have known" the consumer is under 18. A violation is a per se violation of the Maryland Consumer Protection Act. One source says the law reaches processing from April 1, 2026 — [Md. Com. Law 14-4707 (statute PDF, 2026 session web edition)](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2026RS/Statute_Web/gcl/14-4707.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Maryland AG data privacy page](https://oag.maryland.gov/Resources-Info/Pages/data-privacy.aspx) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Manatt (secondary)](https://www.manatt.com/insights/newsletters/privacy-and-data-security/now-in-effect-maryland-law-raises-bar-on-sensitive-data-data-minimization-and-children-s-privacy) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- **Oregon HB 2008 (signed June 3, 2025; effective January 1, 2026):** it bans selling the data of consumers the controller knows (or willfully disregards) are **under 16**, and bans selling anyone's precise geolocation. One Perkins Coie item said under 18; the Oregon bill text shows under 16 — [Oregon HB 2008 enrolled measure](https://apps.oregonlegislature.gov/liz/2025R1/Downloads/MeasureDocument/HB2008) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Kelley Drye, 2025-09 (secondary)](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/ad-law-access/fragmentation-of-privacy-requirements-accelerates-as-four-states-amend-nascent-laws.md) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- **Montana SB 297 (signed May 8, 2025; effective October 1, 2025):** it lowers the threshold to 25,000 Montana consumers, or 15,000 plus more than 25% of revenue from data sales. The minors' sections (9–11) reportedly apply "regardless" of the general threshold, and the cure period was removed — [Shook Hardy (secondary)](https://www.shb.com/intelligence/newsletters/pds/hanson-montana-privacy-law) (accessed 2026-11-21); [TrueVault, 2025-05-12 (secondary)](https://www.truevault.com/learn/montana-updates-its-privacy-law) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Cooley, 2025-10-15 (secondary)](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2025/2025-10-15-the-evolving-state-privacy-landscape-major-updates-to-consumer-privacy-laws-in-montana-and-connecticut) (accessed 2026-11-21).

### Inferences
- A two-person, pre-revenue startup is plainly an SBA "small business". In Texas and Nebraska, the only live rule is: do not sell sensitive data without prior consent. miyagi does not sell data, and its privacy policy should say so.
- The provisions with real present-day reach are the threshold-free minors' rules in Colorado and Montana and, if the reading of SB 1295 is right, Connecticut's threshold-free coverage of sensitive-data processing. Screenshots that incidentally capture health, financial or children's data, plus users aged 13–17, put miyagi near these. The practical duties are: no targeted ads or data sales for minors; no engagement-maximizing design aimed at minors (streak pressure, nag notifications; Connecticut's ban is explicit); a written risk-of-harm review; and data minimization.
- Maryland's "should have known" standard matters later, if miyagi markets to teens and grows past 35,000 Maryland users.

### Gaps
- I did not verify Rhode Island's exact thresholds (I believe 35,000 customers, or 10,000 plus 20% of revenue, but this was not confirmed in this session).
- I did not confirm from enacted text whether Connecticut's minors' provisions (from 2023's SB 3 and 2025's SB 1295) apply below the 35,000 threshold, or exactly how the sensitive-data trigger reads.
- I did not confirm whether Oregon's under-16 ban applies below the OCPA's general thresholds.
- I did not verify the Texas 541.155 penalty amounts or cure period, or the definition of "sensitive data" (541.001).

## 4. Consumer health data laws: Washington MHMDA, Nevada SB 370, Connecticut

### Takeaway
Washington's My Health My Data Act has **no revenue or volume threshold** for a "regulated entity", and it defines "collect" broadly enough ("infer, derive, or otherwise process") that incidental screen capture of health information could arguably bring miyagi in. Nevada's SB 370 likewise has no thresholds but has no private right of action. The risk is real but speculative, and it can be designed down: scope the capture, don't retain, and don't infer.

### Cited Findings
- RCW 19.373.010: "consumer health data" is personal information "linked or reasonably linkable to a consumer" that identifies past, present or future physical or mental health status. "Collect" means to "buy, rent, access, retain, receive, acquire, infer, derive, or otherwise process" consumer health data. "Health care services" includes any service "to assess, measure, improve, or learn about" a person's health. A "regulated entity" is any legal entity that conducts business in Washington or targets Washington consumers and determines the purpose and means of processing, with **no revenue or volume threshold**. "Small business" (under 100,000 consumers, or under 50% of revenue from health data) is a subset of regulated entities, not an exemption — [RCW 19.373.010, Washington Legislature (current)](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.373.010) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- **Nevada SB 370 (effective March 31, 2024):** regulated entities are anyone doing business in Nevada or targeting Nevada consumers, without "processing volume and monetary applicability thresholds". It bars **any person** from selling consumer health data without prior written consent. It has **no private right of action**; violations are deceptive trade practices enforced by the AG — [FPF (secondary)](https://fpf.org/blog/health-data-is-what-health-data-does-in-nevada/) (accessed 2026-11-21); [WilmerHale, 2023-06-14 (secondary)](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20230614-nevada-legislature-passes-consumer-health-data-privacy-bill) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Bass Berry (secondary)](https://www.bassberry.com/news/nevada-consumer-health-data-law-takes-effect-on-march-31-2024/) (accessed 2026-11-21); [SB 370 enrolled text](https://archive.leg.state.nv.us/Session/82nd2023/Bills/SB/SB370.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- Connecticut's 2023 SB 3 imposed restrictions on consumer health data similar to Washington's — [Epstein Becker Green (secondary)](https://www.ebglaw.com/health-law-advisor/nevada-joins-washington-and-connecticut-to-protect-consumer-health-data-privacy) (accessed 2026-11-21).

### Inferences
- A coding-tutorial screen grab can incidentally show a patient-portal tab, a fitness app, a therapy-appointment email, or a code comment about a medical condition. Because "collect" includes "receive", "retain" and "otherwise process", sending that screenshot to an LLM arguably "collects" consumer health data. miyagi would then be a regulated entity for Washington users, with no size floor, and would owe a separate consumer health data privacy policy, consent for collection, and separate consent for sharing (duties as I understand MHMDA; not re-verified this session).
- The defensive position is that miyagi neither seeks nor uses health data and discards incidental captures. The product can make that true: capture only the tutorial video window and the code editor, never the full desktop; block known health, email and messaging windows; avoid retaining raw screenshots; and tell the model to ignore non-code content. Adding a statement to the privacy policy is low cost.
- Washington's private right of action (through the Washington Consumer Protection Act, as I understand it) is the main tail risk. Nevada and Connecticut rely on AG enforcement.

### Gaps
- I did not re-fetch RCW 19.373.090 (private right of action through RCW 19.86) or the separate-policy and consent sections (19.373.020–.030). These are commonly reported but were not verified this session.
- I did not retrieve Connecticut's consumer health data text or confirm whether it covers "any person" regardless of thresholds.
- I found no case applying MHMDA to incidental data capture.

## 5. Breach notification: Cal. Civ. Code 1798.82

### Takeaway
California's breach law applies to **any individual or business** conducting business in California that owns or licenses computerized personal information, regardless of size or CCPA status. Since January 1, 2026, consumer notice is due **within 30 calendar days** of discovery, and the AG must get a sample notice within 15 days if more than 500 Californians are affected. A username or email plus password is covered "personal information", so screenshots or logs that capture credentials, combined with account emails, can create a notifiable breach.

### Cited Findings
- Civ. Code 1798.82(a)(1) covers "an individual or business that conducts business in California, and that owns or licenses computerized data that includes personal information". Notice "shall be made within 30 calendar days of discovery or notification of the data breach" ((a)(2)(A)), with delays allowed for law enforcement needs or to determine scope and restore integrity ((a)(2)(B)). An entity that maintains data it does not own must notify the owner "immediately following discovery" ((b)). If more than 500 California residents are notified, a sample notice goes to the AG within 15 calendar days ((f)). Personal information includes "a username or email address, in combination with a password or security question and answer that would permit access to an online account" ((h)(2)) — [Civ. Code 1798.82 via california.public.law (code text, current)](https://california.public.law/codes/ca_civ_code_section_1798.82) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- The 30-day deadline came from SB 446 (Ch. 319, Stats. 2025), effective January 1, 2026, replacing "most expedient time possible and without unreasonable delay" — [Data Protection Report, 2025-11 (secondary)](https://www.dataprotectionreport.com/2025/11/california-tightens-data-breach-notification-timelines-imposes-30-day-notice-requirement/) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Hunton (secondary)](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/california-updates-data-breach-notification-timeline-requirements) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- The FTC treats weak security at a small edtech company as a Section 5 problem. In Chegg, alleged failures (including storing personal data in cloud databases in plain text) led to four breaches affecting about 40 million people. The 2023 final order requires a security program, limits on data collection and retention, MFA, and user access and deletion rights — [FTC press release, 2022-10-31](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2022/10/ftc-brings-action-against-ed-tech-provider-chegg-careless-security-exposed-personal-data-millions) (accessed 2026-11-21); [FTC final order release, 2023-01](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/01/ftc-finalizes-order-ed-tech-provider-chegg-lax-security-exposed-student-data) (accessed 2026-11-21).

### Inferences
- miyagi stores or relays several kinds of data that would make a breach notifiable: Google-linked email addresses (through Supabase), any stored screenshots or OCR text (which can contain passwords typed in a terminal, `.env` files, API keys, or session tokens visible on screen), and bring-your-own API keys if stored server-side. An API key is not clearly "a password ... for an online account" under (h)(2), but screenshots showing a login form with credentials next to the user's account email could be.
- Every state has its own breach law, keyed to the residence of the affected person. A worldwide user base adds the GDPR and other foreign regimes (out of scope here).
- The cheapest mitigations: never persist raw screenshots server-side (process and discard); scan for and redact secrets on the device before upload; keep BYO keys in the OS keychain, never on the proxy; encrypt at rest; and write a one-page incident plan that can meet the 30-day clock.

### Gaps
- I did not survey the other 49 states' deadlines or definitions.

## 6. Wiretap and eavesdropping (CIPA, SB 690, federal Wiretap Act) and the Video Privacy Protection Act

### Takeaway
SB 690, **signed September 30, 2026**, ends *private* suits under CIPA's "pen register" section (Penal Code 638.51) over website and app conduct. It does **not** touch sections 631 or 632, and its broad "commercial business purpose" exemption was dropped. So the session-replay and chat-wiretap theory under 631 survives. A screen-capture tool that reads third parties' messages displayed on a consenting user's screen raises a novel, untested theory under California's all-party-consent rule; the federal Wiretap Act's one-party consent is friendlier. VPPA exposure looks low because miyagi does not rent, sell or deliver video. Still, the Supreme Court is deciding *Salazar v. Paramount Global* (argued October 14, 2026), which could widen who counts as a "consumer".

### Cited Findings
- **SB 690 (signed 2026-09-30):** amends Penal Code 637.2 to remove the private right of action for alleged 638.51 (pen register and trap-and-trace) violations arising from conduct on "internet websites, online applications, or mobile applications". The AG can still enforce. It applies retroactively to pending claims filed within two years before the operative date, and it "does not legalize the underlying use". Plaintiffs can still bring all-party-consent recording claims and ECPA, common-law privacy and UDAP claims — [WilmerHale, 2026-10-06 (secondary)](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20261006-cipa-reform-signed-into-law) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Morgan Lewis, 2026-10 (secondary)](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/10/california-eliminates-private-right-of-action-under-cipas-pen-register-and-trap-and-trace-provision) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- The legislature removed the earlier "commercial business purpose" exemption from sections 631, 632 and 632.7 before passage. A pre-signing analysis said the law, if signed, takes effect January 1, 2027 — [Sidley, 2026-09 (secondary)](https://www.sidley.com/en/insights/newsupdates/2026/09/californias-sb-690-clears-the-legislature-what-it-means-for-cipa-website-tracking-claims) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Assembly Privacy Committee analysis, 2026-06](https://apcp.assembly.ca.gov/system/files/2026-06/sb-690-caballero-apcp-analysis.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- **Penal Code 631(a)** reaches anyone who, "without the consent of all parties to the communication", reads or learns the contents of a message "while the same is in transit or passing over any wire, line, or cable, or is being sent from, or received at any place within this state". It also reaches anyone who "aids, agrees with, employs, or conspires" with such a person — [Penal Code 631 via california.public.law (code text, current)](https://california.public.law/codes/ca_penal_code_section_631) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- In *Javier v. Assurance IQ* (9th Cir. 2022, unpublished), the court held that section 631 requires **prior** consent of all parties (retroactive consent is not enough) and that a session-replay vendor was plausibly a "third party" rather than an extension of the website. On remand the claim was dismissed as time-barred (2023) — [DLA Piper, 2022-06 (secondary)](https://www.dlapiper.com/en/insights/publications/2022/06/javier-v-assurance-iq-llc-et-al) (accessed 2026-11-21); [N.D. Cal. order, 2023-01, via Leagle](https://www.leagle.com/decision/infdco20230106b44) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- **Federal Wiretap Act, 18 U.S.C. 2511(2)(d):** interception is not unlawful where the interceptor "is a party to the communication or where one of the parties" has given prior consent, unless the interception is "for the purpose of committing any criminal or tortious act" — [18 U.S.C. 2511, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/2511) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- **VPPA, 18 U.S.C. 2710:** a "video tape service provider" is in the business of "rental, sale, or delivery of prerecorded video cassette tapes or similar audio visual materials". A "consumer" is "any renter, purchaser, or subscriber of goods or services from a video tape service provider". Knowingly disclosing personally identifiable information that identifies a person as having requested or obtained specific video materials is barred, unless the consumer gives written consent in a form "distinct and separate" from other legal obligations, either at the time or in advance for up to 2 years, with a chance to withdraw. Damages are actual, "but not less than liquidated damages in an amount of $2,500", plus punitive damages and attorney's fees — [18 U.S.C. 2710, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/2710) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- ***Salazar v. Paramount Global* (No. 25-459):** cert was granted January 26, 2026 on whether "consumer" covers subscribers to any of a video provider's goods or services (here, a newsletter) or only its audiovisual ones. The Sixth Circuit had taken the narrow view. Argument was October 14, 2026, and coverage reported the justices appeared likely to let the suit proceed. **No decision yet** — [Cornell LII cert summary](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/cert/25-459) (accessed 2026-11-21); [SCOTUSblog, 2026-10](https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/10/court-appears-likely-to-allow-internet-suit-based-on-decades-old-video-privacy-law-to-go-forward/) (accessed 2026-11-21); [DLA Piper, 2026-01 (secondary)](https://privacymatters.dlapiper.com/2026/01/supreme-court-to-clarify-meaning-of-consumer-under-vppa/) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- ***Solomon v. Flipps Media*, 136 F.4th 41 (2d Cir. 2025):** the court adopted the "ordinary person" test for personally identifiable information. A Facebook ID plus a video title sent through the Meta Pixel was not information an ordinary person could use to identify the viewer "with little or no effort". En banc review was denied July 24, 2025. A cert petition (No. 25-228) was filed; its outcome was not confirmed — [Morgan Lewis, 2025-07 (secondary)](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2025/07/second-circuit-shuts-the-door-on-meta-pixel-vppa-claims) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Baker Botts (secondary)](https://ourtake.bakerbotts.com/post/102kbml/second-circuit-rules-on-scope-of-vppa-protection) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Solomon cert petition, 2025-08-21](https://www.supremecourt.gov/DocketPDF/25/25-228/370365/20250821140616502_Solomon%20Cert%20Petition%20Final.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-21).

### Inferences
- **Screen capture and third parties:** the miyagi user consents, but a friend whose Discord DM or email appears on screen does not. A plaintiff could argue that miyagi (and, through it, Anthropic or Google) "reads ... the contents" of a message "received at" a place in California without all-party consent, and that the user "aids" that reading. Defenses: messages already shown on screen are arguably no longer "in transit"; miyagi acts as the user's own tool rather than an eavesdropper; and the data is used only to serve the user. No case I found tests a user-installed screen-capture tutor under 631, so this is low-likelihood but high-severity (statutory damages per violation, class actions). Federal law is friendlier because one party (the user) consents, but the "criminal or tortious purpose" carve-out invites state-law-based theories.
- SB 690 mainly removes the *pen-register* theory against miyagi.study and the app's telemetry. The section 631 session-replay theory remains, so avoid session-replay or chat-widget vendors on the site unless prior consent is obtained.
- If miyagi ever captures **microphone audio**, the all-party consent rule for recording confidential communications under Penal Code 632 becomes the main concern. Text-to-speech output is not a problem.
- **VPPA:** miyagi does not rent, sell or deliver the videos; YouTube does. Linking a user's identity to the YouTube video IDs they study, and sending that to vendors, is the pattern VPPA plaintiffs litigate. Two arguments help: miyagi is not plausibly a "video tape service provider", and disclosures to processors serving the user's own request are arguably "request processing" within the ordinary-course exception (2710(a)(2) and (b)(2)(E); not re-verified). If *Salazar* broadens "consumer", the remaining defense is the provider definition. Keep video IDs out of third-party analytics and ad pixels.

## 7. FTC Act Section 5: deception and unfairness, AI claims, AI privacy commitments, and edtech cases

### Takeaway
The current FTC (Chair Ferguson) has narrowed AI enforcement to **deception about AI capabilities and results**. It set aside the Rytr order in December 2025 but kept DoNotPay- and Workado-style cases: unsubstantiated claims that an AI performs as well as a professional, or detects or grades accurately, remain the core risk. The FTC's 2024 guidance still states the law: honor privacy and confidentiality promises, and don't quietly change terms to start using user data for AI training. For miyagi, the risky claims are accuracy figures for auto-grading, learning-outcome promises ("learn twice as fast", "job-ready"), "replaces a tutor or bootcamp", and absolute privacy claims ("we never store your screen") that the architecture doesn't guarantee.

### Cited Findings
- **Operation AI Comply** (announced September 25, 2024) comprised five actions against AI hype and deceptive AI tools. DoNotPay marketed "the world's first robot lawyer" without testing it against human lawyers. Its order, finalized February 11, 2025, requires $193,000, notice to 2021–2023 subscribers, and a ban on claims of substituting for any professional service without evidence — [FTC press release, 2024-09-25](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/09/ftc-announces-crackdown-deceptive-ai-claims-schemes) (accessed 2026-11-21); [FTC final DoNotPay order release, 2025-02](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/02/ftc-finalizes-order-donotpay-prohibits-deceptive-ai-lawyer-claims-imposes-monetary-relief-requires) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- **Rytr:** on December 22, 2025 the FTC reopened and set aside its 2024 consent order. It found the complaint did not allege that Rytr itself made deceptive statements, and it cited the July 2025 AI Action Plan's direction to set aside orders that "unduly burden AI innovation" — [Inside Privacy (secondary)](https://www.insideprivacy.com/united-states/federal-trade-commission/ftc-sets-aside-rytr-final-order-pursuant-to-white-house-ai-action-plan/) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Hogan Lovells (secondary)](https://www.hoganlovells.com/en/publications/in-rare-move-ftc-sets-aside-rytr-order-for-burdening-ai-innovation-and-failing-to-plead-violations) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Holland & Knight, 2026-08 (secondary)](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/08/a-look-at-the-ftcs-current-approach-to-ai-regulation) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- **Current posture (secondary):** the FTC focuses on deceptive or unsubstantiated claims about AI capabilities, earnings and performance, not on restricting tools because they could be misused. Matters include a final order against **Workado** for misrepresenting the accuracy of its AI content detector (barring unsupported accuracy claims) and **Air AI** (an $18M proposed judgment over earnings and refund claims). On **July 1, 2026** the FTC issued a *Proposed Policy Statement Concerning the Suppression of Accuracy in AI Systems*. It frames AI liability as deception, including distorting outputs toward undisclosed objectives, and requires clear and conspicuous disclosures — [Holland & Knight, 2026-08-19 (secondary)](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/08/a-look-at-the-ftcs-current-approach-to-ai-regulation) (accessed 2026-11-21); [FTC AI topic page](https://www.ftc.gov/industry/artificial-intelligence) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- **January 2024 guidance:** model-as-a-service companies that fail to honor privacy commitments may violate FTC law. Commitments count wherever they appear (marketing, terms of service, marketplaces). The FTC has required deletion of models and algorithms built on unlawfully obtained data — [FTC Tech Blog, "AI Companies: Uphold Your Privacy and Confidentiality Commitments", 2024-01](https://www.ftc.gov/policy/advocacy-research/tech-at-ftc/2024/01/ai-companies-uphold-your-privacy-confidentiality-commitments) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- **February 2024 guidance:** adopting more permissive data practices (for example, using data for AI training) through a "surreptitious, retroactive amendment" to terms or the privacy policy may be unfair or deceptive (citing *Gateway Learning*, 2004) — [FTC Tech Blog, 2024-02](https://www.ftc.gov/policy/advocacy-research/tech-at-ftc/2024/02/ai-other-companies-quietly-changing-your-terms-service-could-be-unfair-or-deceptive) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- **Edtech cases:** Chegg (data security; complaint 2022, final order 2023; see Q5). Chegg also agreed to pay $7.5M in September 2025 over cancellation practices. Edmodo (May 22, 2023): children's data was used for advertising and COPPA compliance was outsourced to schools. The order bars requiring more data than an educational activity needs and imposes a $6M penalty, suspended for inability to pay — [FTC Chegg cancellation release, 2025-09](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/09/ed-tech-provider-chegg-pay-75-million-settle-ftc-allegations-concerning-unlawful-cancellation) (accessed 2026-11-21); [FTC Edmodo release, 2023-05-22](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/05/ftc-says-ed-tech-provider-edmodo-unlawfully-used-childrens-personal-information-advertising) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- The FTC business blog post "Keep your AI claims in check" (February 2023) is the agency's checklist: don't exaggerate what the AI does, don't claim it beats non-AI products without proof, and know the risks. Its URL was not re-verified in this session: [FTC business blog, 2023-02](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2023/02/keep-your-ai-claims-check) (accessed 2026-11-21; unverified link).

### Inferences
- **Higher-risk claims for miyagi:** numeric grading accuracy ("99% accurate") without a test set; "AI tutor as good as a human mentor"; outcome claims ("land a dev job", "learn 3x faster") without data; "private", "nothing leaves your computer" or "we never store screenshots" when images go to Render, Anthropic and Google; and "free forever" if caps or paywalls are planned. **Lower-risk:** describing what the product does, plus a visible note that AI grading can be wrong.
- **Shared lesson cache and training:** if user screenshots, code or tutorial context ever feed the shared per-video cache or model training, decide and disclose that now. A later switch without fresh opt-in fits the February 2024 "quiet terms change" theory. Confirm and state that the Anthropic and Gemini API terms do not use API inputs for training (vendor terms not checked here).
- FTC action against a two-person free app is unlikely, but a consent order would last about 20 years. State AGs and UCL/CLRA plaintiffs use the same deception standard.

### Gaps
- I did not verify the Workado and Air AI order dates, the status of the July 2026 proposed accuracy policy statement, or the Evolv (November 2024) case from primary FTC releases.

## 8. Subscriptions, if paid plans come: the FTC Negative Option rule and California's Automatic Renewal Law

### Takeaway
The FTC's 2024 "click-to-cancel" rule was **vacated** by the Eighth Circuit on July 8, 2025, on procedural grounds. The FTC restarted rulemaking with an ANPRM in March 2026; no new rule is final. ROSCA and Section 5 enforcement continue. California's ARL, as amended by AB 2863 (effective July 1, 2025), already requires most of what the federal rule did: express affirmative consent, easy online cancellation, annual reminders, and notice of price changes.

### Cited Findings
- The Eighth Circuit vacated the whole Negative Option Rule on July 8, 2025, because the FTC skipped a required preliminary regulatory analysis (FTC Act Section 22). It did not reach the substantive challenges — [WilmerHale, 2025-08-01 (secondary)](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/sitecore/content/whlaunch/home/research/news-publications/20250801-eighth-circuit-vacates-the-ftcs-click-to-cancel-rule-but-federal-and-state-regulators-likely-to-remain-active) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Latham (secondary)](https://www.lw.com/en/insights/eighth-circuit-vacates-ftc-click-to-cancel-rule-days-before-compliance-deadline) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- The FTC announced an ANPRM in March 2026, with comments due April 13, 2026. It asks whether to amend the 1973 rule, revive parts of the vacated rule, or treat industries differently. No NPRM was reported. Enforcement continues under ROSCA and Section 5 (for example, NextMed, Care.com at $8.5M, and Amazon at $2.5B) — [Jones Day, 2026-05 (secondary)](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/05/ftc-revives-clicktocancel-rule-new-risks-for-subscription-businesses) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Gibson Dunn, 2026-02 (secondary)](https://gibsondunn.com/ftc-restarts-negative-option-rulemaking-after-eighth-circuit-vacatur-enforcement-under-rosca-continues/) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- California AB 2863 was signed September 24, 2024 and took effect July 1, 2025. It amends Bus. & Prof. Code 17601–17602 to require "express affirmative consent" to the automatic renewal, annual reminders, clear and conspicuous notice of material price or service changes, and a "click to cancel" option, and it extends the law to free-to-pay conversions — [Barnes & Thornburg, 2025 (secondary)](https://btlaw.com/en/insights/alerts/2025/california-expands-automatic-renewal-law-new-requirements-now-in-effect) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Davis Wright Tremaine, 2024-10 (secondary)](https://dwt.com/insights/2024/10/ab-2863-updates-california-automatic-renewal-law) (accessed 2026-11-21); [AB 2863 bill text, LegiScan](https://legiscan.com/CA/text/AB2863/id/3012746) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- About 30 states have automatic-renewal or negative-option laws — [Jones Day, 2026-05 (secondary)](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/05/ftc-revives-clicktocancel-rule-new-risks-for-subscription-businesses) (accessed 2026-11-21).

### Inferences
- None of this applies while miyagi is free. Before launching paid plans: use an unticked consent checkbox for auto-renewal next to the price and terms; send a confirmation email with the terms and how to cancel; offer one-click online cancellation in the same medium; send an annual reminder; give advance notice of price changes; and keep consent records. Payment processors such as Stripe can handle much of this. A free-trial-to-paid conversion is covered.

## 9. State AI laws in force or scheduled by November 2026 that could touch an AI tutoring and grading app

### Takeaway
None of the state AI laws currently imposes heavy duties on a free AI coding tutor:
- **Colorado** repealed and replaced its AI Act with SB 26-189 (signed May 14, 2026; effective **January 1, 2027**). It now regulates automated decision-making for consequential decisions; AG-only enforcement.
- **Utah** requires AI disclosure mainly when a user asks, plus in "high-risk" interactions.
- **California's AI Transparency Act** lost its 1-million-user threshold through SB 1000 (enacted September 30, 2026), but its duties concern image, video and audio provenance.
- **California SB 243** (companion chatbots, in effect since January 1, 2026) is the one with a **private right of action ($1,000 per violation)**. A coding tutor should fall within its exclusions as long as it is not designed to sustain a social relationship.
- **Texas TRAIGA** (effective January 1, 2026) bans only intentional harmful uses; AG-only enforcement with a 60-day cure.

### Cited Findings
- **Colorado:** SB25B-004 (signed August 28, 2025) moved SB 24-205's effective date from February 1, 2026 to June 30, 2026 — [Akin (secondary)](https://www.akingump.com/en/insights/ai-law-and-regulation-tracker/colorado-postpones-implementation-of-colorado-ai-act-sb-24-205) (accessed 2026-11-21). SB 26-189, signed May 14, 2026, **repeals and replaces** SB 24-205 effective **January 1, 2027**. It replaces "high-risk AI system" regulation with a framework for ADMT used in consequential decisions, drops many procedural duties such as impact assessments, adds HIPAA and FDA carve-outs, and keeps AG-only enforcement with no private right of action. Context: a December 2025 executive order targeted SB 205, and an AI developer's April 2026 federal constitutional challenge drew DOJ intervention — [Goodwin, 2026-06 (secondary)](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/06/alerts-technology-fs-colorado-enacts-law-repealing-replacing-landmark-ai-act) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Holland & Knight, 2026-05-18 (secondary)](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/05/colorado-governor-signs-sb-189) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Davis Wright Tremaine, 2026-05 (secondary)](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2026/05/colorado-ai-act-repeal-new-transparency-law) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- **Utah AI Policy Act as amended by SB 226 (2025):** disclosure of generative AI is required only after a "clear and unambiguous request" to know whether the user is talking to a human, and in specified high-risk interactions. A safe harbor covers clear and conspicuous disclosure at the start of or throughout an interaction. SB 332 extended the Act's repeal date to July 2027 — [SB 226 enrolled text, Utah Legislature](https://le.utah.gov/Session/2025/bills/enrolled/SB0226.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-21); [FPF overview, 2025-04 (secondary)](https://fpf.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/Overview-of-Utahs-2025-Enacted-AI-Legislation.pdf) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Davis Polk (secondary)](https://www.davispolk.com/index%2Ephp/insights/client-update/utah-scales-back-reach-generative-ai-consumer-protection-law) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- **California AI Transparency Act (SB 942):** originally covered providers of GenAI systems with over 1,000,000 monthly users. AB 853 delayed operation to August 2, 2026 — [Justia, Bus. & Prof. Code 22757.1](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-bpc/division-8/chapter-25/section-22757-1) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Morgan Lewis, 2026-08 (secondary)](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/08/new-california-ai-disclosure-rules-become-operative) (accessed 2026-11-21). **SB 1000, enacted September 30, 2026 and effective immediately**, extends the Act to any person that produces a publicly accessible GenAI system in California regardless of user count. - **California SB 243 (companion chatbots; signed October 13, 2025; in effect since January 1, 2026):**
  - **Definition:** an AI system with a natural-language interface giving "adaptive, human-like responses" that can meet a user's social needs, shows anthropomorphic features, and can sustain a relationship across interactions.
  - **Exclusions:** bots used solely for customer service, business operations, productivity, analysis of source information, internal research or technical assistance; video-game bots limited to the game; stand-alone consumer devices.
  - **Duties:** clear AI disclosure where a reasonable person could be misled; a suicide and self-harm protocol with crisis referrals, published on the operator's website; for known minors, AI disclosure, break reminders at least every 3 hours, and measures against sexual content.
  - **Reporting** to the Office of Suicide Prevention starts July 1, 2027.
  - **Private right of action:** the greater of actual damages or **$1,000 per violation**, plus fees.
  - Practitioners warn that ongoing dialogue, personalization or emotional support could take a bot outside the exclusions — [Skadden, 2025-10 (secondary)](https://skadden.com/insights/publications/2025/10/new-california-companion-chatbot-law) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Crowell (secondary)](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/californias-chatbot-bill-may-impose-substantial-compliance-burdens-on-many-companies-deploying-ai-assistants) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Perkins Coie (secondary)](https://legacy.perkinscoie.com/insights/update/california-companion-chatbot-law-now-effect) (accessed 2026-11-21); [SB 243 chaptered text, LegiScan](https://legiscan.com/CA/text/SB243/2025) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- **Texas TRAIGA (HB 149; effective January 1, 2026):**
  - **Prohibitions:** private-sector prohibitions require intent, for example systems built to incite self-harm, violence or crime, or to produce CSAM or sexual deepfakes. A developer or deployer is not liable merely because a third party misuses the system.
  - **Enforcement:** AG only, no private right of action, a 60-day cure period.
  - **Penalties:** reported tiers vary by source ($10k–$12k curable; $80k–$200k uncurable; $2k–$40k per day continuing) — [Greenberg Traurig, 2025-06 (secondary)](https://gtlaw.com/en/insights/2025/6/traiga-key-provisions-of-texas-new-artificial-intelligence-governance-act) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Latham (secondary)](https://www.lw.com/en/insights/2025/06/texas-signs-responsible-ai-governance-act-into-law) (accessed 2026-11-21); [IAPP (secondary)](https://iapp.org/news/a/governor-signs-texas-responsible-artificial-intelligence-governance-act) (accessed 2026-11-21).

### Inferences
- **SB 243:** a code-focused lab grader with a "sensei" voice is best treated as a "productivity / technical assistance" tool. Risk rises if the persona remembers personal details across sessions, offers encouragement as emotional support, or invites off-topic chat. Cheap hedges: a persistent "AI" label; a system-prompt rule that redirects non-coding personal topics; a crisis-resource fallback if a user expresses self-harm; and no relationship framing in marketing. Of all the AI laws here, this one has a plaintiff's damages hook.
- **CATA after SB 1000:** the duties concern provenance of image, video and audio content. miyagi generates text lessons and uses the system voice (not generative TTS), so it likely has nothing to do. Revisit if miyagi adds generated images, video or AI voices. Whether a wrapper of Claude or Gemini "produces" a GenAI system is unsettled (there are now licensee duties).
- **Colorado SB 26-189 (from 2027):** applies to ADMT used for consequential decisions. Practice-lab grades are not consequential unless miyagi grades feed certificates, admissions or hiring. Avoid marketing grades as credentials without review.
- **Utah:** a standing "you're talking to an AI" disclosure gives safe harbor. **TRAIGA:** essentially no duties beyond not building harmful systems.
- The December 2025 federal executive order and DOJ intervention signal federal pressure against state AI laws. Federal preemption has not been enacted, so the state laws stand.

## 10. Accessibility: ADA Title III, apps and websites, and California's Unruh Act

### Takeaway
Whether ADA Title III covers a purely online or desktop product is unsettled. The Ninth Circuit (which covers California) requires a "nexus" to a physical place, and a California Court of Appeal held in 2022 that an online-only retailer is not covered under the ADA or Unruh. Web accessibility filings are high (3,117 federal suits in 2025, up 27%), but they cluster on e-commerce and businesses with physical locations. Realistic risk for a free desktop app and a static marketing site is low, and basic WCAG 2.1 AA conformance on the site and app is a cheap hedge.

### Cited Findings
- *Robles v. Domino's* (9th Cir.): a website or app is covered when there is a sufficient nexus to a physical place. *Gil v. Winn-Dixie* (11th Cir. 2021): a website itself is not a place of public accommodation. The Second Circuit has not decided the question, and its district courts are split. Title III has no regulatory standard for websites or apps — [ABA GPSolo (secondary)](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/gpsolo/publications/gpsolo_ereport/2022/february-2022/title-iii-americans-disabilities-act-website-compliance/) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Crowell on *Gil* (secondary)](https://www.crowell.com/NewsEvents/AlertsNewsletters/all/Website-Wars-Eleventh-Circuit-Rules-in-a-Split-Decision-That-Websites-are-Not-Public-Accommodations-for-Purposes-of-the-Americans-With-Disabilities-Act) (accessed 2026-11-21); [ABA Business Law Today, 2025-08 (secondary)](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business%5Flaw/resources/business-law-today/2025-august/digital-accessibility-under-title-iii-ada/) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- A California Court of Appeal panel held that an online retailer without a physical location is not liable for an inaccessible website under the ADA or the Unruh Act (2022). Unruh provides the greater of $4,000 or actual damages per proven violation — [Clark Hill (secondary)](https://www.clarkhill.com/news-events/news/california-court-of-appeal-holds-that-solely-online-retailers-without-a-physical-location-are-not-liable-for-inaccessible-websites-under-the-ada-and-californias-unruh-civil-rights-act/) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Kelley Drye (secondary)](https://kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/podcasts/ad-law-access/california-appellate-court-holds-in-favor-of-a-digital-only-retailer-under-both-ada-and-unruh-act) (accessed 2026-11-21).
- Per Seyfarth's March 2026 analysis (reported secondhand), 3,117 federal website accessibility suits were filed in 2025, up 27% from 2024. DOJ filed a Statement of Interest on February 2, 2026 opposing a proposed website-accessibility class settlement. A California bill to require WCAG 2.1 AA for sites and apps offering goods or services in the state remained in committee in early 2026 — [Deque (secondary, citing Seyfarth)](https://www.deque.com/blog/the-unruh-act-understand-the-lawsuits-and-how-state-and-federal-regulations-could-combine-to-create-blockbuster-settlements/) (accessed 2026-11-21); [Mondaq (secondary)](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/discrimination-disability-sexual-harassment/1748962/doj-throws-wrench-into-proposed-ada-website-accessibility-class-settlement) (accessed 2026-11-21).

### Inferences
- miyagi has no physical location, is free and is based in California, so a California ADA or Unruh claim faces the nexus and online-only holdings. New York federal courts are the plaintiff-friendly venue for online-only claims. Risk stays low because filers target companies that can pay settlements.
- Cheap hedges: WCAG 2.1 AA on miyagi.study (contrast, alt text, keyboard navigation, captions on any demo video); keyboard-operable and screen-reader-labeled overlay controls; and an accessibility statement with a contact email.

- I did not determine the current DOJ Title III position on web accessibility.

## Implications for miyagi

### Takeaway
Today, the laws that clearly apply regardless of size are: **CalOPPA and Delaware's DOPPA** (post an accurate privacy policy); **FTC Act Section 5 and state UDAP and UCL law** (don't over-claim AI accuracy, outcomes or privacy); **state breach-notice laws** (30 days in California); the **threshold-free minors' rules in Colorado, Montana and probably Connecticut**; **Texas and Nebraska's ban on selling sensitive data without consent**; and **SB 243**, if the tutor drifts toward companionship. The biggest low-probability, high-severity tails come from the screen capture itself: CIPA 631 all-party consent for third-party messages, and Washington MHMDA for incidental health data, both with private plaintiffs. Most risk can be engineered away with capture scoping, on-device redaction and no retention, which also makes the privacy policy easy to write truthfully.

### Cited Findings
- See the sources in sections 1–10. Each item below is an inference built on them.

### Inferences
Each item gives the risk, who could act, likelihood, severity, mitigation, and whether a lawyer is needed.

1. **Missing or inaccurate privacy policy (CalOPPA 22575, Delaware 1205C).**
   - **Who could act:** California AG (UCL), Delaware DOJ, and the FTC if the policy is false.
   - **Likelihood:** low, because of the 30-day cure. **Severity:** low to moderate.
   - **Mitigation:** post the policy on miyagi.study and link it in the app (sign-in screen and settings). Include the six 22575(b) items: data categories (Google account ID and email, screenshots, code, tutorial or video ID, telemetry, optional BYO keys); third-party categories (AI model providers, auth and database host, cloud host, site host, telemetry vendor); review and change process; change-notice process; effective date; a Do Not Track statement (for example, "we do not track across sites, so we do not respond to DNT"); and whether third parties collect cross-site data (they should not). Add retention periods and a contact address.
   - **Lawyer:** a review is advisable but not required. Founders can draft it; a lawyer should check it for consistency with actual data flows.
2. **Deceptive AI or privacy claims (FTC Section 5, state UDAP, UCL and CLRA).**
   - **Who could act:** FTC, state AGs, private class plaintiffs.
   - **Likelihood:** low to moderate as marketing grows. **Severity:** moderate (about a 20-year consent order, redress).
   - **Mitigation:** keep a claims-substantiation file (grading accuracy measured on a held-out set of labs, with the method described). Avoid outcome promises and "as good as a human tutor". Show a visible "AI grading can be wrong" note. Never say "nothing leaves your computer" or "we never store your screen" unless the code enforces it. Avoid "free forever" if paid tiers are planned.
   - **Lawyer:** not needed for everyday copy. Get a quick review before publishing any numeric accuracy or outcome claim.
3. **Repurposing user data for the shared lesson cache or for training without consent (FTC February 2024 "quiet terms change" theory).**
   - **Who could act:** FTC, AGs. **Likelihood:** low. **Severity:** moderate (possible deletion of models or data).
   - **Mitigation:** decide now what enters the shared per-video cache. It should be generated lesson content derived from the public video, not user screenshots or code. Make any use of user content opt-in, and notify users and get fresh consent before broadening use. Confirm in writing (vendor terms) that API inputs to Anthropic and Google are not used for training, and say so accurately.
   - **Lawyer:** no, unless the data model changes.
4. **Screen capture sweeping in third parties' communications (CIPA 631 and 632; Wiretap Act 2511 tortious-purpose carve-out).**
   - **Who could act:** private plaintiffs (class actions), the AG.
   - **Likelihood:** low (novel theory, no case found against user-installed tools). **Severity:** high (statutory damages per violation times class size).
   - **Mitigation:** capture only the tutorial window and the code editor or terminal, never the full desktop. Keep a default deny-list for messaging, email, banking, health and password-manager apps. Show a visible capture indicator and a pause hotkey. Get prior, specific consent at onboarding. Never capture microphone audio. Don't retain raw images server-side. Tell the model to ignore non-code content. Avoid session-replay or chat-widget vendors on miyagi.study (SB 690 removed only the pen-register theory).
   - **Lawyer:** yes, before scaling or before any paid launch. The consent-flow design and the 631 "third party or extension of the user" analysis are fact-specific, and plaintiffs' firms actively litigate CIPA.
5. **Incidental health data (Washington MHMDA, Nevada SB 370, Connecticut).**
   - **Who could act:** Washington private plaintiffs and AG; Nevada and Connecticut AGs.
   - **Likelihood:** low. **Severity:** high in Washington.
   - **Mitigation:** the same capture scoping as item 4. A policy statement that miyagi does not seek or infer health data and discards incidental captures. No retention. Never sell any data. If miyagi ever processes health data on purpose, it needs a separate MHMDA consumer-health-data policy and consent.
   - **Lawyer:** a short consult is advisable, because MHMDA's breadth and private right of action are unusual.
6. **Data breach involving credentials or secrets visible in screenshots (Civ. Code 1798.82 and other states' laws; FTC Chegg-type unfairness; 1798.150 does not apply while miyagi is not a "business").**
   - **Who could act:** affected users (negligence and UCL), the AG, the FTC.
   - **Likelihood:** low to moderate (startups get breached). **Severity:** moderate to high.
   - **Mitigation:** process screenshots in memory and discard them. Redact secrets on the device before upload (`.env` files, key patterns, password fields). Store BYO keys in the OS keychain only, never on the Render proxy. Use Supabase row-level security. Encrypt at rest and keep short log retention. Write a one-page incident plan with the 30-day California clock and the AG 15-day sample notice for more than 500 residents.
   - **Lawyer:** only if a breach happens. Then yes, immediately, for the multistate notice analysis.
7. **VPPA (video IDs linked to user identity sent to vendors).**
   - **Who could act:** private class plaintiffs ($2,500 per person).
   - **Likelihood:** low (miyagi is not plausibly a "video tape service provider"). **Severity:** high if it ever applied.
   - **Mitigation:** no ad pixels (Meta or Google Ads) on the site or app. Keep video IDs out of third-party analytics, or pseudonymize telemetry. Watch the *Salazar v. Paramount* decision (expected by about June 2027).
   - **Lawyer:** no, unless miyagi starts hosting or streaming video itself.
8. **State comprehensive privacy laws, below thresholds.**
   - **Who could act:** state AGs (Colorado also through district attorneys).
   - **Likelihood:** low. **Severity:** low to moderate.
   - **Mitigation:** never sell personal data, which settles Texas and Nebraska. No targeted advertising, especially to anyone under 18 (Maryland, Connecticut, Oregon, Colorado). No engagement-maximizing dark patterns aimed at minors, such as streak shaming or nag notifications (Connecticut's design-feature ban; Colorado's duty of care). Write a short internal risk-of-harm note for the 13–17 audience. Recheck coverage at about 25,000 users in any single state.
   - **Lawyer:** no for now. Yes when approaching 25,000–35,000 users in a state, or before adding ads.
9. **CCPA becoming applicable later.**
   - **Who could act:** CPPA, AG, breach plaintiffs.
   - **Likelihood:** remote now. Coverage would come from adding ad-tech "sharing" at 100,000 Californians, or from revenue over $26.6M.
   - **Mitigation:** avoid cross-context ad tech. Design capture to avoid sensitive personal information now, which is cheap insurance against the 2026 regulations' risk-assessment and ADMT duties.
   - **Lawyer:** at scale.
10. **SB 243 companion-chatbot creep.**
    - **Who could act:** private plaintiffs ($1,000 per violation) and the AG.
    - **Likelihood:** low if the tutor stays code-scoped. **Severity:** moderate.
    - **Mitigation:** a persistent AI label; redirect personal or emotional topics; a crisis-resource fallback; no relationship or "friend" marketing; no long-term personal memory beyond learning progress.
    - **Lawyer:** a brief review of the tutor persona is advisable because the exclusions are untested.
11. **Other AI laws (Utah AIPA, Texas TRAIGA, CATA after SB 1000, Colorado SB 26-189 from 2027).**
    - **Who could act:** AGs only. **Likelihood:** very low. **Severity:** low.
    - **Mitigation:** a standing AI disclosure (Utah safe harbor). Don't generate image, video or audio content without revisiting CATA. Don't let grades feed credentials, admissions or hiring without reviewing Colorado's ADMT rules.
    - **Lawyer:** no.
12. **Future paid plans (California ARL under AB 2863; ROSCA; FTC ANPRM).**
    - **Who could act:** FTC, AGs, ARL and UCL class plaintiffs.
    - **Likelihood:** moderate once billing exists. Subscription cancellation is a top enforcement area (Chegg paid $7.5M in 2025).
    - **Mitigation:** an unticked auto-renew consent box next to the price; a confirmation email; one-click online cancellation; annual reminders; price-change notices; consent records.
    - **Lawyer:** yes, a one-time checkout and terms review before charging money.
13. **Accessibility (ADA Title III, Unruh).**
    - **Who could act:** private plaintiffs. **Likelihood:** low. **Severity:** low to moderate ($4,000 Unruh minimum).
    - **Mitigation:** WCAG 2.1 AA on miyagi.study; keyboard and screen-reader support in the overlay; an accessibility contact.
    - **Lawyer:** no.
14. **No legal entity; minor founders.**
    - **Who could act:** any claimant. Without an entity, claims run against the founders personally.
    - **Likelihood:** low. **Severity:** potentially high (personal exposure; capacity and contract issues for minors signing vendor terms).
    - **Mitigation:** form an entity (an LLC or Delaware C-corp, with a parent or guardian involved as needed). Put vendor accounts (Anthropic, Google, Supabase, Render) and the terms of service and privacy policy in the entity's name.
    - **Lawyer:** yes. Entity formation and minors' capacity to contract are outside this research and need counsel.
