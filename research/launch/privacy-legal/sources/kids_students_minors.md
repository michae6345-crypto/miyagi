## 1. COPPA as amended in 2025: definitions, "directed to children", actual knowledge, age screening, personal information, new duties, penalties

### Takeaway
COPPA covers miyagi only if (a) the service is "directed to children" under 13, judged by a multi-factor test, or (b) miyagi has actual knowledge that a particular user is under 13. A general-audience coding tool with teen users and teen founders is not child-directed on those facts alone. A neutral age screen, deletion on discovery, and marketing that stays at 13+ keep it outside COPPA. The 2025 amendments (effective 23 June 2025, compliance by 22 April 2026) add duties that apply only to covered operators: separate consent for non-integral third-party disclosures, a written retention policy, and a written security program. They are still the model for teen handling. The maximum civil penalty is $53,088 per violation. It was not raised for 2026.

### Cited Findings
**Dates and status**
- The FTC press release finalizing the amendments is dated 16 January 2025. It says the rule takes effect "60 days after its publication in the Federal Register" and gives entities "one year from that publication date" to comply. — [FTC press release, 16 Jan 2025](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-finalizes-changes-childrens-privacy-rule-limiting-companies-ability-monetize-kids-data), accessed 2026-11-21
- Federal Register final rule (published 22 April 2025): "The amended Rule is effective June 23, 2025," and "regulated entities have until April 22, 2026 to comply," with exceptions for some Safe Harbor provisions (§ 312.11(d)(1), (d)(4), (g)). — [90 FR, 22 Apr 2025, FR Doc. 2025-05904 (govinfo)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-04-22/html/2025-05904.htm), accessed 2026-11-21
- The FTC COPPA FAQ carries a banner: "The COPPA Rule was amended on April 22, 2025. Review the revised Rule for the most up-to-date requirements". The FAQ body still describes the 2013 rule (A.1), so treat it as guidance that has not been fully updated. — [FTC, Complying with COPPA: FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions), accessed 2026-11-21

**"Directed to children", mixed audience, general audience** (16 C.F.R. § 312.2)
- Factors the FTC weighs: "subject matter, visual content, use of animated characters or child-oriented activities and incentives, music or other audio content, age of models, presence of child celebrities or celebrities who appeal to children," and whether advertising on or promoting the service is child-directed. The 2025 text adds "marketing or promotional materials or plans, representations to consumers or to third parties, reviews by users or third parties, and the age of users on similar websites," plus "competent and reliable empirical evidence regarding audience composition." — [16 C.F.R. § 312.2 (LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/312.2), accessed 2026-11-21
- Under the mixed-audience paragraph, a child-directed service that "does not target children as its primary audience" and age-screens before collecting personal information "shall not be deemed directed to children with regard to any visitor not identified as under 13." — [16 C.F.R. § 312.2 (LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/312.2), accessed 2026-11-21
- FAQ D.4: "a general audience site does not become 'mixed audience' just because some children use the site or service." FAQ D.6: a mixed-audience site "may age screen your users, but you may not block children from participating altogether." A general-audience site, by contrast, "may block children from participating" (H.3). — [FTC COPPA FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions), accessed 2026-11-21

**Actual knowledge**
- COPPA covers general-audience operators "only where such operators have actual knowledge" that they are collecting personal information from a child under 13 (A.12). The Rule does not require operators "to investigate the ages of visitors" (H.1). Actual knowledge can arise from an age screen, from a parent's report, or from monitoring or alerts (H.1, H.6). — [FTC COPPA FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions), accessed 2026-11-21
- The 2025 Federal Register text keeps the actual-knowledge framing for general-audience sites ("that have actual knowledge they are collecting personal information from children"). I found nothing in the part I read that adopts a constructive-knowledge standard. — [90 FR, 22 Apr 2025 (govinfo)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-04-22/html/2025-05904.htm), accessed 2026-11-21

**Neutral age screening**
- 2025 rule preamble: age screening "must be done in a neutral manner that does not default to a set age or encourage visitors to falsify age information." — [90 FR, 22 Apr 2025 (govinfo)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-04-22/html/2025-05904.htm), accessed 2026-11-21
- FAQ H.3 says to "Ask age information in a neutral manner at the point at which you invite visitors to provide personal information" and to avoid "encouraging children to falsify their age information." It warns against signalling the cutoff "by stating that visitors under 13 cannot participate or should ask their parents before participating." It also says "simply including a check box stating, 'I am over 12 years old' would not be considered a neutral age-screening mechanism." — [FTC COPPA FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions), accessed 2026-11-21
- On back-button retries, FAQ H.3/D.7: "FTC staff recommends using a cookie to prevent children from back-buttoning to enter a different age." The entry also should not default "to an age 13 or over" (D.7). — [FTC COPPA FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions), accessed 2026-11-21

**When you learn a user is under 13**
- FAQ H.6: "the operator must either meet COPPA's notice and parental consent requirements or delete the child's information." For a one-time contact, the operator must "delete the child's online contact information from your records promptly after you send your response" (H.4). — [FTC COPPA FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions), accessed 2026-11-21
- FTC Enforcement Policy Statement, 25 Feb 2026: the FTC will not bring a COPPA action against an operator that collects, uses or discloses personal information solely to determine a user's age without prior parental consent, on conditions. The data must be used only for age verification and kept no longer than needed, then deleted promptly. Vendors must be bound to confidentiality and deletion. Notice must be clear, security appropriate and the method reasonably accurate. The relief applies only if the operator otherwise complies with COPPA. (Summary from Greenberg Traurig, 4 Mar 2026; primary PDF linked there.) — [GT Alert, 4 Mar 2026](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2026/3/ftc-signals-relaxed-coppa-enforcement-for-age-verification-technologies); [FTC press release, 25 Feb 2026](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/02/ftc-issues-coppa-policy-statement-incentivize-use-age-verification-technologies-protect-children); [FTC policy statement PDF](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/coppa-age-verification-policy-statement.pdf), all accessed 2026-11-21

**What counts as personal information** (§ 312.2)
- Enumerated items include "A persistent identifier that can be used to recognize a user over time and across different websites" (item 7), "A photograph, video, or audio file where such file contains a child's image or voice" (item 8), "A biometric identifier that can be used for the automated or semi-automated recognition of an individual" (item 10, new in 2025) and "A government-issued identifier" (item 6, new in 2025). — [16 C.F.R. § 312.2 (LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/312.2), accessed 2026-11-21; [FTC press release, 16 Jan 2025](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-finalizes-changes-childrens-privacy-rule-limiting-companies-ability-monetize-kids-data), accessed 2026-11-21
- The 2025 rule clarified that information collected for "support for internal operations" "may be used or disclosed to carry out those activities." — [90 FR, 22 Apr 2025 (govinfo)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-04-22/html/2025-05904.htm), accessed 2026-11-21

**New 2025 duties (apply to covered operators)**
- Separate consent: operators must obtain "separate verifiable parental consent" before disclosing children's personal information to third parties for targeted advertising "or other purposes." — [FTC press release, 16 Jan 2025](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-finalizes-changes-childrens-privacy-rule-limiting-companies-ability-monetize-kids-data), accessed 2026-11-21
- Law-firm summaries say the separate-consent duty does not cover disclosures "integral" to the service. They say advertising, data-broker sharing and AI training are not integral and therefore need separate consent. I did not verify this in the Federal Register text. — [Search-result summary citing Goodwin, DLA Piper, Loeb 2025 alerts](https://goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/01/alerts-practices-dpc-ftc-issues-long-awaited-new-coppa-rules), accessed 2026-11-21
- Then-Chair Khan's statement tied the retention limits to AI models that "feed on data". — [Statement of Chair Khan, 16 Jan 2025](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/statement-of-chair-lina-m-khan-re-coppa-amendments-1-16-2025.pdf) (via search summary), accessed 2026-11-21
- Retention (§ 312.10): personal information may be kept "for only as long as is reasonably necessary to fulfill the specific purpose(s) for which the information was collected" and "may not be retained indefinitely." Operators must "establish, implement, and maintain a written data retention policy" that states the purposes, the business need and a deletion timeframe, and must disclose it in the online notice. — [16 C.F.R. § 312.10 (LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/312.10), accessed 2026-11-21
- Security (§ 312.8): operators must "establish, implement, and maintain a written information security program". It must name one or more coordinating employees, include risk assessments at least annually, safeguards, regular testing and monitoring, and an annual evaluation. Before handing children's data to third parties, operators must "obtain written assurances" that those parties will protect it. — [16 C.F.R. § 312.8 (LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/312.8), accessed 2026-11-21
- The FTC declined to finalize proposed limits on push notifications and proposed edtech or school-authorization rule changes. — [FTC press release, 16 Jan 2025](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-finalizes-changes-childrens-privacy-rule-limiting-companies-ability-monetize-kids-data), accessed 2026-11-21

**Civil penalty**
- The 2025 inflation adjustment raised FTC maximum civil penalties, including COPPA, to $53,088 per violation. — [FTC adjustments, 90 FR, 17 Jan 2025](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-01-17/html/2025-01361.htm), accessed 2026-11-21
- For 2026, a search summary reports that OMB Memorandum M-26-11 (17 April 2026) issued no new cost-of-living multiplier, so agencies keep the 2025 amounts. A 2026 FTC Federal Register notice exists. I did not read it directly. — [FR public inspection 2026-18853](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-18853.pdf); [FMC rule, 91 FR, 2 Jun 2026](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-06-02/html/2026-10996.htm), accessed 2026-11-21

### Inferences
- The factors cut against child-directedness for miyagi: coding subject matter, YouTube programming tutorials and teen founders marketing to high-school peers. Teen peers are 13+, and COPPA is about under-13s. The 2025 additions on "marketing or promotional materials or plans" and "representations to ... third parties" mean pitch decks, school outreach and social posts are evidence. Avoid "for kids", middle-school or elementary outreach, cartoonish child-oriented visuals and under-13 influencers. A bonsai brand is not child-oriented in itself.
- Screenshots are a COPPA concern only for a user under 13, and they can contain the child's image (webcam, video calls, photos on screen), name, email or other identifiers. miyagi's voice is text-to-speech output, so it does not collect the child's voice unless a microphone is added. The Supabase user ID and any device ID are persistent identifiers. The Google sign-in email is online contact information.
- Sending under-13 data to Anthropic or Google would be a third-party disclosure. Whether it is "integral" (grading) or not (any provider training) matters. On Gemini's free tier, Google trains on the data and humans review it (see section 6), which would be non-integral.
- miyagi does not strictly owe the 2025 written retention policy and security program while it is a general-audience service that blocks under-13s. They are cheap and they make a strong record for teen data and for school deals. Write both.

- The eCFR was blocked (redirect to unblock.federalregister.gov). I used LII, and LII does not show an amendment date. The text of §§ 312.8 and 312.10 matches the 2025 amendments.

## 2. FTC enforcement against edtech, games and AI (2022-2026) and the AI-chatbot inquiry

### Takeaway
Recent FTC/DOJ cases target ed-tech that relied on schools for consent (Edmodo), default-on teen communications (Epic), child-appealing games (Cognosphere), mislabeled kid content (Disney), third-party SDKs leaking kids' data (Apitor) and apps that knowingly took under-13 data (Sendit, NGL). The largest 2026 resolution is DOJ's $400M TikTok COPPA settlement. In September 2025 the FTC opened a 6(b) study of AI companion chatbots and minors.

### Cited Findings
- **Epic Games (Dec 2022):** $275M COPPA penalty, then the largest for an FTC rule violation, plus $245M in refunds in a separate case. The FTC also called default-on voice and text chat for children and teens an unfair practice. The order requires voice and text to be off by default, with opt-in for under-13s and for teens 13-17. — [DOJ press release](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/epic-games-inc-developer-fortnite-video-game-agrees-275-million-penalty-and-injunction); [IAPP, "teen privacy arrives at the FTC"](https://iapp.org/news/a/takeaways-from-epic-games-settlement-teen-privacy-arrives-at-the-ftc), accessed 2026-11-21

The order bars Edmodo from relying on schools as consent intermediaries unless the required arrangements are in place. — [FTC press release, May 2023](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/05/ftc-says-ed-tech-provider-edmodo-unlawfully-used-childrens-personal-information-advertising); [Covington Inside Privacy](https://www.insideprivacy.com/childrens-privacy/ftc-announces-coppa-settlement-against-ed-tech-provider-including-strict-data-minimization-and-data-retention-requirements/), accessed 2026-11-21

### Inferences
- Epic shows the FTC treats teen (13-17) harms under Section 5 unfairness even where COPPA does not apply. Defaults matter for teens: no open chat or sharing, and private by default.
- Apitor shows that third-party SDKs (analytics, crash reporting) count as disclosures. miyagi's Electron app should not embed ad or analytics SDKs that send data on their own.
- Edmodo shows that if miyagi later goes into schools and relies on school consent for under-13s, it must give schools full notice and keep data within school purposes.

## 3. Federal bills: COPPA 2.0, KOSA and the KIDS Act (status Nov 2026)

### Takeaway
No new federal kids' privacy or safety statute had been enacted as of the latest sources I found. The House passed the KIDS Act (H.R. 7757) on 29 June 2026. It packages a KOSA variant without a duty of care, COPPA 2.0-style teen protections and AI-chatbot provisions. It was referred to Senate Commerce on 13 July 2026, and Senate sponsors oppose it.

### Cited Findings
- H.R. 7757, the Kids Internet and Digital Safety Act, passed the House 267-117 on 29 June 2026. Its latest recorded action is a 13 July 2026 referral to Senate Commerce. The Senate's own KOSA bill (S. 1748) remains in committee, and the bill needs 60 Senate votes. — [CRS Legal Sidebar LSB11465, 3 Aug 2026](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/LSB11465.html); [Axios, 29 June 2026](https://axios.com/2026/06/29/house-vote-senate-clash-kids-online-safety), accessed 2026-11-21
- The KIDS Act extends COPPA 2.0-style protections to teens, bans targeted advertising to minors, requires the most protective defaults, restricts addictive features, addresses AI chatbots accessed by minors, includes age-verification incentives and preempts weaker state laws. It omits KOSA's duty of care, and Sen. Blumenthal said "KOSA without a duty of care isn't KOSA." — [Crowell client alert, 2026](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/house-advances-bipartisan-kids-online-safety-bill-but-senate-showdown-looms); [Pillsbury on KIDS Act AI chatbots](https://www.pillsburylaw.com/en/news-and-insights/kids-act-house-ai-chatbot-safety.html), accessed 2026-11-21
- Loeb (June 2026): "Congress continues to try—and fail—to enact children's privacy legislation." — [Loeb & Loeb, June 2026](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2026/06/childrens-online-privacy-2026-state-app-store-design-code-and-social-media-laws), accessed 2026-11-21

### Inferences
- If a KIDS-Act-style law passes, it would bring 13-16-year-olds under COPPA-like rules and AI-chatbot duties. That would directly hit miyagi's teen base. Design the teen experience now so it already meets "most protective default" and no-targeted-ads rules.

## 4. App-store and OS age-signal laws (Utah, Texas, Louisiana, Alabama, California AB 1043/AB 1856)

### Takeaway
Texas SB 2420 is in force: the Fifth Circuit stayed the district-court injunction in May-June 2026, and the Supreme Court declined to vacate the stay. It and the Utah, Louisiana and Alabama laws run through app stores serving mobile devices. They likely do not reach a desktop app downloaded directly from miyagi's site. California's Digital Age Assurance Act (AB 1043, amended by AB 1856) is different. It runs through operating-system providers on any "general-purpose computing device". From 1 Jan 2027 it requires developers to request an age-bracket signal when their app is downloaded and launched. That likely reaches miyagi's macOS and Windows app whether or not it ships through a store, and it gives miyagi actual knowledge of under-13 users.

### Cited Findings
**Texas SB 2420 (App Store Accountability Act)**
- Original effective date 1 Jan 2026. Judge Pitman (W.D. Tex.) granted a preliminary injunction on 23 Dec 2025, finding the law "likely unconstitutional". — [CCIA case page](https://ccianet.org/litigation/ccia-v-paxton/); [Texas Tribune, 23 Dec 2025](https://www.texastribune.org/2025/12/23/texas-app-store-child-ban-age-verification/), accessed 2026-11-21
- Timeline: Pitman denied a stay in May 2026. The Fifth Circuit granted an administrative stay on 28 May 2026 and formally stayed the injunction in June 2026; a 4 June 2026 order applied intermediate scrutiny. CCIA's emergency application to the Supreme Court (No. 25A1390) was denied in July 2026, so the stay stands and the merits appeal (5th Cir. No. 25-51073) is pending. The Supreme Court denial comes from CCIA's own page and a search summary that dates it 6 July 2026. — [CCIA case page](https://ccianet.org/litigation/ccia-v-paxton/); [TPR, 28 May 2026](https://www.tpr.org/news/2026-05-28/texas-app-age-verification-law-allowed-to-go-into-effect-for-now); [SCOTUSblog, June 2026](https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/06/texas-asks-court-to-leave-in-place-age-verification-and-parental-consent-law-on-apps/); [CCIA emergency application](https://www.supremecourt.gov/DocketPDF/25/25A1390/413071/20260610230907810_CCIA%20Emergency%20Application%20for%20Vacatur.pdf), accessed 2026-11-21
- Developer duties (Morrison Foerster, 10 June 2026): assign an age rating and disclose what drove it; notify the store of significant changes (new data practices, monetization, rating or material functional change) so it can renew parental consent; verify each user's age category and consent status using store data; use store-provided data only for age enforcement, legal compliance and safety defaults, then delete it. Parental consent is collected by the store. The law is enforced under the Texas DTPA. The alert refers to users of a "mobile device" and treats a developer as one whose app is available through an app store. — [MoFo, updated 10 June 2026](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/251111-texas-targets-app-stores-with-new-accountability-law), accessed 2026-11-21

**Utah SB 142 as amended by HB 498 (2026)**
- HB 498, signed 18 March 2026, removed AG enforcement, leaving "the only enforcement mechanism" as a private right of action. It pushed the key provisions to 6 May 2027. CCIA sued on 5 Feb 2026 and dismissed voluntarily on 21 April 2026 after the AG said it "could not and would not enforce" the law. Developers must verify age category and get consent through the app stores. — [Loeb, May 2026](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/passle/2026/05/update-on-utah-app-store-law--another-waiting-game); [CCIA v. Brown page](https://ccianet.org/litigation/ccia-v-brown/), accessed 2026-11-21

**Louisiana HB 570 and Alabama**
- Louisiana's 1 July 2026 effective date was delayed to 1 July 2027 by a bill the governor signed. Alabama's law takes effect 1 Jan 2027 and covers new and existing accounts. As of June 2026, none of the four state app-store laws was yet in effect (Texas then took effect after the stay). — [Loeb, June 2026](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2026/06/childrens-online-privacy-2026-state-app-store-design-code-and-social-media-laws), accessed 2026-11-21

**California AB 1043 (Digital Age Assurance Act), as amended by AB 1856**
- AB 1043 was signed 13 Oct 2025 and is operative 1 Jan 2027. An "operating system provider" is one that "develops, licenses, or controls the operating system software" on "a computer, mobile device, or other general-purpose computing device". A "developer" is a person who "owns, maintains, or controls an application." Developers must request an age-bracket signal from the OS provider or covered app store when the app is downloaded and launched. A developer that receives a signal is deemed to have actual knowledge of the age range and must not willfully disregard clear and convincing contrary information. Penalties are up to $2,500 per affected child (negligent) and $7,500 (intentional), enforced by the AG only, with no private right of action. — [National Law Review, 28 Oct 2025](https://natlawreview.com/article/california-introduces-new-age-verification-requirements-software-applications), accessed 2026-11-21
- The age brackets are under 13, 13 to under 16, 16 to under 18, and 18+. OS providers have a good-faith safe harbour and developers have none (Kelley Drye FAQ, via search summary; the page was blocked to direct fetch). — [Kelley Drye FAQ](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/ad-law-access/faq-digital-age-assurance-act-california-youth-safety-on-the-internet), accessed 2026-11-21
- AB 1856 (Ch. 184, Stats. 2026) was signed 10 Sept 2026. It amends Civ. Code §§ 1798.500-1798.504: a developer that receives a signal has "actual knowledge" of the user's age range "across all platforms of that app". Companies may not request age signals unless a law requires it. Enforcement is AG only, with the same penalties. — [Fenwick, 9 Oct 2026](https://www.fenwick.com/insights/publications/california-enacts-comprehensive-child-safety-laws-targeting-ai-chatbots); [search summary of Freshfields/Didit/Phoronix](https://phoronix.com/news/California-AB-1856-Passes), accessed 2026-11-21
- EFF (29 May 2026, Assembly version) says AB 1856 adds "browser providers and website operators to the list of entities that must comply" and exempts open-source OS distributors. The final enacted scope may differ. — [EFF, 29 May 2026](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/05/one-step-forward-two-steps-back-cas-ab-1856-exempts-open-source-expands-age-gating), accessed 2026-11-21

### Inferences
- Texas, Utah, Louisiana and Alabama: miyagi distributed by direct download on Mac or Windows likely falls outside, because the duties attach to apps made available through an "app store" for mobile devices. A Microsoft Store or Mac App Store listing could change that if a store counts as an "app store" under a given statute. The Texas definition as summarized is tied to mobile devices, so desktop stores are probably outside. Re-check before any store launch.
- California AB 1043/1856 is the age-signal law most likely to bind miyagi. It covers general-purpose computers, so macOS and Windows qualify, and the developer duty is tied to download and launch, not to a store. From 1 Jan 2027 miyagi should request the OS age-bracket signal on launch, if Apple and Microsoft expose one on desktop. A "13 to under 16" or "under 13" signal then fixes knowledge, and an under-13 signal triggers COPPA (delete or get consent). A user's self-reported "18+" does not override an OS signal saying otherwise.

## 5. State kids' design codes (California, Maryland, Vermont, Nebraska and others)

### Takeaway
On 12 March 2026 the Ninth Circuit narrowed the injunction against California's AADC (AB 2273). California then repealed and replaced the AADC with AB 2246 (signed 10 Sept 2026, generally effective 1 Jan 2027). AB 2246 covers services "likely to be accessed by children" (under 18) and requires age estimation or child protections for all users, high-privacy defaults, no default profiling or precise geolocation, and no data-extracting dark patterns. Maryland's code is in force and unenjoined. Vermont (1 Jan 2027) has no minimum size threshold. Nebraska (1 Jan 2026) dropped its revenue-share threshold in July 2026. Whether a tiny free app is covered turns on each law's "business" definition, which is unconfirmed for AB 2246 and Nebraska as amended.

### Cited Findings
**California**
- *NetChoice v. Bonta*, No. 25-2366 (9th Cir. 12 Mar 2026), 170 F.4th 744: the court narrowed the injunction. Still enjoined: DPIAs, notice-and-cure, the data-use restrictions in § 1798.99.31(b)(1)-(4) and dark patterns in (b)(7). The age-estimation and default-privacy-settings injunctions were vacated, and the case was remanded. — [Cooley, 24 Mar 2026](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-03-30-netchoice-v-bonta-ninth-circuit-narrows-injunction-against-californias-ageappropriate-design-code-act); [9th Cir. opinion PDF](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2026/03/12/25-2366.pdf), accessed 2026-11-21. Common Sense statement](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/press-releases/statement-on-netchoice-v-bonta-ruling), accessed 2026-11-21
- AB 2246, signed 10 Sept 2026, "repeals and replaces" the AADC. It applies to "businesses that provide an online service, product, or feature likely to be accessed by children" ("any consumer under 18"). The "likely to be accessed" factors include: "directed to children as defined by" COPPA, "routinely accessed by a significant number of children", child-directed ads, "design elements that are known to be of interest to children", and internal research. **Maryland Kids Code**
- Effective 1 Oct 2024; covers children under 18. In *NetChoice v. Brown* (D. Md.) the motion to dismiss was denied on 24 Nov 2025, and in July 2026 the court allowed a dormant Commerce Clause claim. ## 6. FERPA, SOPIPA and other student-privacy laws; what a school deal would require

### Takeaway
FERPA binds schools that receive federal funds, not vendors directly. A vendor is reached only when a school discloses education records to it as a "school official" under the school's direct control. Students who download miyagi on their own create no education records, so FERPA does not apply. SOPIPA and Illinois SOPPA apply to operators whose product is designed and marketed for, and used primarily for, K-12 school purposes, and general-audience apps are carved out. Marketing miyagi to high schools as a classroom tool could pull it in. A school deal would mean a DPA, usually the Student Data Privacy Consortium's National DPA with state exhibits.

### Cited Findings
- An outside party is a school official only if it "Performs an institutional service or function for which the agency or institution would otherwise use employees", "Is under the direct control of the agency or institution with respect to the use and maintenance of education records", follows § 99.33(a) use and redisclosure limits, and "Meets the criteria specified in the school or local educational agency's (LEA's) annual notification." — [ED Student Privacy Policy Office FAQ](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/faq/who-school-official-under-ferpa), accessed 2026-11-21
- ED guidance materials (PTAC, 2014) say direct control means the school governs maintenance, use, retention and deletion. Non-educational uses such as product development may defeat the exception, and a written agreement is best practice. (Taken from search snippets of the guidance.) — [ED, Protecting Student Privacy While Using Online Educational Services (Feb 2014)](https://tech.ed.gov/files/2014/09/Student-Privacy-and-Online-Educational-Services-February-2014.pdf); [ED, Responsibilities of Third-Party Service Providers under FERPA](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/resources/responsibilities-third-party-service-providers-under-ferpa), accessed 2026-11-21
- SOPIPA (Bus. & Prof. Code § 22584): an "operator" is one with actual knowledge that the site, service or app "is used primarily for K–12 school purposes and was designed and marketed for K–12 school purposes". The section does not apply to "general audience" websites, services, applications or mobile apps. FPF says applicability turns on design, purpose and marketing, not on whether a contract exists. — [California Public Law, § 22584](https://california.public.law/codes/business_and_professions_code_section_22584); [FPF SOPIPA materials](https://fpf.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/SOPIPA-At-a-Glance.pdf), accessed 2026-11-21

(From a district FAQ found via search.) — [search result: district SOPPA FAQ (Google Doc export)](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1O7rR-1lSQft3LUWfxuYdZYB8oqvriZyap_LOI7XTo2A/export?format=pdf), accessed 2026-11-21
- NY Education Law § 2-d and 8 NYCRR Part 121 (effective Jan 2020): third-party contractors receiving student PII must sign contracts covering purpose, security, expiration and return or deletion, plus the Parents' Bill of Rights. NYSED's statewide SDPC membership lets NY districts use the NDPA. — [NY Educ. ### Inferences
- **Today:** no school contracts and individual downloads, so FERPA and Ed. Code 49073.1 (which governs LEA contracts) do not apply. Under SOPIPA and SOPPA, miyagi is general audience, built around YouTube tutorials for anyone.
- **Trigger risk:** a teacher-facing dashboard, "for your CS class" messaging, school-domain sign-in, assignment features or a high-school sales push could make miyagi "designed and marketed for K-12 school purposes." SOPIPA then bars targeted ads, profiles and sales, and requires security and deletion on request. For teens using it on their own, keep the positioning general audience ("learn to code from any tutorial").

## 8. AI-safety duties for minors (California SB 243, SB 1119 and related) and whether a coding tutor qualifies

### Takeaway
SB 243 (effective 1 Jan 2026) regulates "companion chatbots". It excludes bots used only for customer service, business operations, productivity and analysis tied to source information, internal research or technical assistance. SB 1119 ("Adam's Law", signed 10 Sept 2026, most provisions effective 1 Jan 2027) adds heavy duties for companion-chatbot operators that permit child users and removes SB 243's actual-knowledge triggers. A task-focused coding grader fits the "technical assistance / productivity" exclusion. A friend-like mentor persona that sustains a relationship across sessions would erode it.

### Cited Findings
- SB 243 defines companion chatbots as systems with adaptive, human-like responses that meet social needs, "including by exhibiting anthropomorphic features and being able to sustain a relationship across multiple interactions". It excludes bots "used solely for customer service, business operations," and certain game bots and devices. Operators must give a "clear and conspicuous notification that the chatbot is artificially generated and not human". For known minors they must give notices "at least every three hours" to take a break and take "reasonable measures to prevent chatbots from producing visual material of sexually explicit conduct". Operators must keep and publish suicide and self-harm protocols, report annually to the Office of Suicide Prevention from 1 July 2027, and face a private right of action with a "minimum $1,000 per violation". — [Skadden, 17 Oct 2025](https://skadden.com/insights/publications/2025/10/new-california-companion-chatbot-law), accessed 2026-11-21
- The exclusion covers a bot "used only for customer service, a business' operational purposes, productivity and analysis related to source information, internal research, or technical assistance". ### Inferences
- miyagi's AI grades code against a tutorial and explains errors. That is "technical assistance" and "productivity and analysis related to source information" (the tutorial and the user's code), so it is likely not a companion chatbot. Keep it that way:
  - no persistent persona memory of the user's personal life;
  - no "how are you feeling" check-ins;
  - no relationship framing or streak guilt.

## Implications for miyagi

### Takeaway
The single clear vendor breach is **Google's Gemini API terms**: under-18 users are barred, and so is an account holder under 18. Fix it before launch by removing Gemini or restricting it to verified adults. Everything else can be managed with a neutral 13+ gate, delete-on-discovery for under-13s, private and minimal teen defaults, AI disclosure, and marketing discipline. Prepare for 1 Jan 2027 (California AB 1043/1856 age signals and AB 2246 design code; Vermont). Get a lawyer before any school deal, any store launch in Texas-type regimes, and for founder contracting capacity.

### Cited Findings
- All findings above, especially: [Gemini API Additional Terms](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/terms); [Anthropic minors guidelines](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/9307344-responsible-use-of-anthropic-s-models-guidelines-for-organizations-serving-minors); [FTC COPPA FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions); [National Law Review on AB 1043](https://natlawreview.com/article/california-introduces-new-age-verification-requirements-software-applications); [Hunton on AB 2246](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/california-enacts-new-law-governing-online-services-accessed-by-children); [Fenwick on SB 1119/AB 1856](https://www.fenwick.com/insights/publications/california-enacts-comprehensive-child-safety-laws-targeting-ai-chatbots). ### Inferences

**What a defensible 13+ gate and teen experience looks like**

1. **Order.** Show "About you" (age) before Google OAuth completes, or at least before any Supabase profile row, screenshot or code is stored. If OAuth must come first, hold the session in memory until the age step passes. The FTC FAQ says to ask age "at the point at which you invite visitors to provide personal information."
2. **Neutral entry.**
   - Ask for month and year of birth (or a full date) with empty pickers, no default and no pre-filled year.
   - Do not say "you must be 13", do not use an "I am 13+" checkbox and do not put the cutoff in the copy or the error. A failure message should be generic, such as "miyagi isn't available for this account right now."
   - Do not hint at the cutoff with a sliding year list that starts at 2013.
   - Keep the screen in miyagi's minimal brand style. Do not explain the threshold.
3. **What to store.** Store an age band (13-15, 16-17, 18+, matching the California AB 1043 brackets) plus the month and year of attestation, so the band can roll forward. Do not keep the full date of birth. For under-13 results, store nothing tied to the person.
4. **Retry block.**
   - On an under-13 result, write a local flag that survives restarts: app-data file plus OS keychain or credential store. This is the desktop equivalent of the FAQ's cookie. The gate then shows the same generic message and does not offer re-entry.
   - Delete any OAuth tokens and server records already created.
   - A hashed Google-subject blocklist is tempting, but it retains a child's persistent identifier. Under the FTC's Feb 2026 policy statement, age-verification data must be limited and deleted promptly, so ask a lawyer before adopting one.
5. **Discovery after the gate.** Provide a "report an underage user" and parent contact route. On credible notice (a parent, or the user's own statements in screenshots or code comments), disable the account and delete server data, provider logs where possible, and screenshots within a set short window. Log the deletion without keeping the personal information.
6. **Teen (13-17) defaults.** Apply these to everyone if possible. That is simpler, and it satisfies AB 2246's "apply to all" alternative.
   - AI providers: Claude via the Commercial API, which does not train on Customer Content. No Gemini free tier, and ideally no Gemini at all.
   - Screenshots: processed in memory, not stored server-side beyond the grading request, with a short fixed retention for logs.
   - No public profile, leaderboard, chat or sharing (the Epic lesson).
   - No ads, no third-party analytics or ad SDKs (the Apitor lesson), no precise geolocation, no profiling, no nudges to share more.
   - No marketing email to teens without opt-in. NY CDPA allows processing beyond what is "strictly necessary" only with the teen's informed consent.
   - Show "You're working with an AI tutor" in-app (Anthropic "must").
   - Use content moderation plus Anthropic's child-safety system prompt if available.
   - Add a crisis-resource response for self-harm text.
   - Add an in-app "report this AI output" control (Microsoft 11.16, Anthropic monitoring).
7. **Documents.** Treat the COPPA §§ 312.8/312.10 elements as the standard for teen data, even though they strictly bind only covered operators:
   - privacy policy with a teen section;
   - written data retention policy (purposes, need, deletion timeframes) published in the privacy notice;
   - written information security program (named owner, annual risk assessment, safeguards, testing, annual review);
   - vendor written assurances;
   - public statement on COPPA compliance and teen safeguards (Anthropic "should");
   - marketing guideline: 13+ only, no under-13 outreach or child-oriented creative.
8. **Parental consent for 13-17.**
   - Not required for a general-audience desktop app today.
   - It arises via the app-store laws (Texas in force for mobile app stores; Utah, Louisiana and Alabama in 2027) if miyagi ships through a covered store.
   - It arises under SB 1119 only if miyagi were a companion chatbot.
   - It would arise under future federal law (KIDS Act, pending) if enacted.
   - It arises under school contracts.
