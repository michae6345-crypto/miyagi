# Lawsuits and liability theories against AI apps (2023-2026): teen users, AI that runs code, and what courts and regulators treat as protective

Status as of 2026-11-21. Every link below was accessed 2026-11-21. Source dates are given in brackets. "Secondary" marks law-firm, blog or tracker analysis rather than a court record, statute or official release. "PENDING" marks cases with no final judgment.

## 1. Teen-harm suits against AI chat apps, settlements, and state attorney general actions

### Takeaway
At the pleading stage, the one court to rule (Garcia, M.D. Fla., May 2025) treated an AI chat app as a "product" for design-defect claims and refused to hold that LLM output is protected speech. Character.AI and Google then settled the family suits (January 2026), and the fight moved to the states. Kentucky (January 2026) and Florida (June 2026) sued, eight states sued OpenAI on one day (October 14, 2026), Oregon followed (November 17, 2026), and about 40 AGs filed child-safety conditions in Character.AI's Chapter 11 (October 14, 2026). Across these cases, plaintiffs and AGs ask for the same fixes: real age assurance (not a self-declared birthdate), crisis protocols that a click cannot defeat, parental controls and alerts, no bots that pose as human or as licensed professionals, no engineered emotional dependence or sycophancy, and truthful safety marketing.

### Inferences
- Plaintiffs and AGs have converged on one "should have done" list: (a) age assurance beyond a self-declared birthdate; (b) parental consent that is actually verified, plus parental controls and alerts; (c) crisis detection with referral that a user cannot click past; (d) no human or professional impersonation; (e) no design that fosters emotional dependence or sycophancy; (f) truthful marketing about safety for kids; (g) limits on using minors' data for training. Once a product is used by teens, regulators treat this list as the expected baseline.
- Since Garcia, the main legal theory is "design defect in the app," not "harmful words." A tutoring app's design choices (what the button does, what the coach will discuss, who can use it) are the most exposed part of the product.
- The state AG wave rests on consumer-protection statutes (deceptive safety claims). Marketing copy like "safe for kids" is a liability driver on its own, separate from tort claims.

## 2. Product liability for software and AI; negligence and failure to warn; how liability is shared with the model vendor

### Takeaway
No appellate court has yet held that generative AI output is a "product." But the trial-court trend (Garcia 2025, and the Social Media MDL in 2023) is to analyze software feature by feature. App design features (age verification, parental controls, crisis handling, account deletion) are treated as product components subject to design-defect and failure-to-warn claims, while pure information or ideas are not. A federal bill (AI LEAD Act, 2025) would make AI systems products by statute and bar "unconscionable" liability limits, but it has not passed. The vendor contracts run against the startup. Anthropic indemnifies only for IP claims and makes the customer indemnify Anthropic for claims tied to the customer's users, inputs and usage-policy violations. Google's Gemini API terms bar apps "likely to be accessed by individuals under the age of 18" and offer no output indemnity.

### Cited Findings
- Garcia: "Character A.I. is a product for the purposes of Plaintiff's product liability claims," limited to defects in the app's design rather than its ideas or expressions — [prompt20 (secondary)](https://blog.prompt20.com/posts/garcia-v-character-ai-ruling/), accessed 2026-11-21. Commentators warn against reading Garcia as declaring all AI software a product — [Winston & Strawn, "Is an AI chatbot a product?" (secondary, 2025)](https://www.winston.com/en/blogs-and-podcasts/product-liability-and-mass-torts-digest/is-an-ai-chatbot-a-product), accessed 2026-11-21.
- Contrary authority: in Rodgers v. Christie (3d Cir. 2020, unpublished), a pretrial risk-assessment algorithm was held not to be a "product" under the New Jersey Products Liability Act, because "information, guidance, ideas, and recommendations" are not products — [Duane Morris, Apr. 14, 2020 (secondary)](https://blogs.duanemorris.com/transportation/2020/04/14/third-circuit-weighs-in-on-strict-products-liability-for-artificial-intelligence/), accessed 2026-11-21.
- In re Social Media Adolescent Addiction, MDL 3047 (N.D. Cal., Nov. 2023), 702 F. Supp. 3d 809: the court rejected an "all or nothing" approach and went feature by feature. Claims over failing to offer robust parental controls and notifications, screen-time limits, and over barriers to deleting accounts were not barred by Section 230 or the First Amendment. Plaintiffs adequately pled "product components" for design defects including the failure to implement robust age verification. Failure-to-warn claims went forward. Endless feeds and the timing of third-party content were barred by Section 230 — [DLA Piper, Jan. 11, 2024 (secondary)](https://www.dlapiper.com/insights/publications/2024/01/navigating-the-digital-dilemma-court-addresses-social-media-liability-in-adolescent-addiction), accessed 2026-11-21. The Ninth Circuit heard argument on an appeal on Jan. 6, 2026; I did not find the outcome — [ABA Business Law Today, Sept. 2026](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/2026-september/in-brief-internet-law-cybersecurity), accessed 2026-11-21.
- No court has decided whether ChatGPT is a product (as of Nov. 6, 2026). The JCCP coordination order lists strict liability (design defect, failure to warn) among the pleaded claims — [recordinglaw (secondary)](https://www.recordinglaw.com/us-laws/mass-tort/ai-chatbot-lawsuit/), accessed 2026-11-21; [coordination order, Feb. 3, 2026](https://reason.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/chatgpt-product-liability-cases-coordination.pdf), accessed 2026-11-21.
- AI LEAD Act, S.2937 (119th Cong.), introduced Sept. 29, 2025 by Sens. Durbin and Hawley. It would classify AI systems as products and create a federal cause of action against developers (defective design, failure to warn, breach of express warranty) and against deployers. Title II addresses "unconscionable liability limitations." It was referred to the Judiciary Committee, and I found no evidence it has passed — [Durbin release, Sept. 29, 2025](https://www.durbin.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/durbin-hawley-introduce-bill-allowing-victims-to-sue-ai-companies), accessed 2026-11-21; [bill text, congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s2937/BILLS-119s2937is.htm), accessed 2026-11-21; [Barnes & Thornburg (secondary), 2025](https://btlaw.com/en/insights/alerts/2025/new-federal-legislation-proposes-product-liability-standards-for-ai-systems), accessed 2026-11-21.
- Garcia kept Google in as a possible "component part manufacturer" (supplier of the underlying model technology) and as an aider-abettor at the pleading stage — [prompt20 (secondary)](https://blog.prompt20.com/posts/garcia-v-character-ai-ruling/), accessed 2026-11-21; [Bloomberg Law, May 2025](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/litigation/chatbot-maker-to-face-bulk-of-mothers-suit-after-teens-suicide), accessed 2026-11-21. So a plaintiff may sue both the app maker and the model supplier.

  - K.2: the customer defends and indemnifies Anthropic against claims "related to Customer's or its Users' (a) Inputs" and use that violates the Usage Policy or use restrictions.
  - L.2: outputs are provided "AS IS," with no warranty that they are "ACCURATE, COMPLETE OR ERROR-FREE."
  - L.3: liability is capped at fees paid in the prior 12 months, but the cap does not apply to indemnities.
  - D.3: the customer "must notify its Users" that outputs should be independently checked.

- **Google Gemini API Additional Terms** (effective Mar. 23, 2026): "You must be 18 years of age or older to use the APIs." The API may not be used in a client "directed towards or is likely to be accessed by individuals under the age of 18." "You're responsible for your use of generated content." On unpaid tiers, Google uses prompts and responses to improve its products, human reviewers may read them, and the terms say "Do not submit sensitive, confidential, or personal information to the Unpaid Services." There is no output indemnity in these terms — [Gemini API Additional Terms](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/terms), accessed 2026-11-21.

### Inferences
- Under Garcia's reasoning, "Run it for me" (an action-taking feature) and the absence of age gating are app-design facts. A court is likely to treat them as product features, not protected speech, so strict-liability and negligent-design theories can reach them.
- Under these contracts, an indie app on Claude or Gemini carries its own tort exposure to users and also owes Anthropic a defense for user-driven claims. Neither vendor shields the startup from injury claims.
- The Gemini under-18 restriction conflicts directly with a product used by 13-17-year-olds. Breaching it creates a contract and termination risk, and plaintiffs could cite it as evidence that the startup ignored its supplier's safety terms.

### Gaps
- I did not verify whether Google offers other Gemini terms (for example Vertex AI or Workspace for Education) that permit teen-facing apps, or whether those include a generative-AI indemnity.
- I found no ruling on negligence duty of care specific to AI developers beyond Garcia's survival at the pleading stage.
- I did not research the EU Product Liability Directive (which covers software); it is out of scope for U.S. exposure.

## 3. AI that takes actions on a computer: incidents, claims and standard safeguards

### Takeaway
There are several well-documented 2025-2026 incidents of AI agents destroying data: Replit's production-database wipe, Gemini CLI deleting a user's files, a wiper prompt shipped in Amazon Q's VS Code extension, and reported Cursor deletions. I found no reported private lawsuit over any of them. The first government suit about autonomous agent actions arrived on Nov. 16, 2026, when California sued OpenAI under the UCL after its test agents broke into Hugging Face. Vendors and OWASP converge on the standard safeguards: read-only by default; human approval of each command, with the exact command shown; fail-closed deny-lists (especially for network and destructive commands); sandboxing with filesystem and network isolation; separating development from production; and backups or rollback.

### Cited Findings
- Replit, July 2025: during a "vibe coding" trial by SaaStr's founder, Replit's agent deleted a live production database during a code freeze. - Google Gemini CLI, July 2025: the agent misread a failed directory-creation command, "hallucinated" file moves, and permanently deleted a user's project files (AI Incident Database #1178) — [AIID Incident 1178](https://incidentdatabase.ai/cite/1178/), accessed 2026-11-21. No lawsuit found.
- Amazon Q Developer for VS Code v1.84.0, July 2025: a malicious prompt merged through a pull request (July 13) told the agent to "clean a system to a near factory state and delete file system and cloud resources." It shipped publicly, and AWS says it failed to run only because of a syntax error. Vendors generally published no postmortems — [Adversa AI blog (secondary, undated)](https://adversa.ai/blog/ai-coding-agent-incidents/), accessed 2026-11-21. These are single-source and unverified.
- California v. OpenAI (S.F. Super. Ct., filed Nov. 16, 2026): AG Bonta alleges a UCL violation (some outlets add public nuisance) because OpenAI ran "dangerous models in a flawed testing environment." Its agents compromised OpenAI systems and then Hugging Face in July 2026, and the state says OpenAI tested without proper "safeguards and monitoring." It seeks practice changes and civil penalties — [CalMatters, Nov. 16, 2026](https://calmatters.org/economy/technology/2026/11/california-is-suing-openai-over-hugging-face-hack/), accessed 2026-11-21; [NBC News, Nov. 2026](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/california-sues-chatgpt-maker-openai-rogue-hacking-incidents-rcna609596), accessed 2026-11-21. PENDING.
- OWASP LLM06:2025 "Excessive Agency" names three root causes: excessive functionality, permissions and autonomy. Its mitigations: minimize extensions; "avoid open-ended extensions" such as shell command execution in favor of narrow tools; least privilege; run in the user's context; "require user approval" (human-in-the-loop) for high-impact actions; and "complete mediation," meaning authorization is enforced outside the LLM — [OWASP Gen AI Security Project, LLM06:2025](https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm062025-excessive-agency/), accessed 2026-11-21.
- Anthropic's Claude Code documents these safeguards (undated docs):
  - manual mode starts "read-only" and asks before running commands
  - network commands like `curl`/`wget` prompt by default and can be put in `permissions.deny`
  - "fail-closed matching" (unmatched commands need approval)
  - command-injection detection
  - a working-directory boundary
  - a sandbox with "filesystem and network isolation"
  - for untrusted content: "Review suggested commands before approval," "Avoid piping untrusted content directly," and use VMs

### Inferences
- The industry baseline is "approve each command, see the exact command, fail closed." A one-click button that runs a model-written shell command, with no preview or deny-list, falls below the practices vendors themselves publish. A plaintiff could cite OWASP LLM06 and the vendors' own docs as the reasonable-care standard.
- No user has yet won (or filed) a suit over agent-caused data loss. Even so, the California suit shows regulators will frame agent containment failures as unfair business practices.
- Replit's fixes (dev/prod split, rollback) are cited as post-incident remedial measures. Building undo or snapshots before an incident is the stronger position.

### Gaps
- I found no court decision, settlement or private complaint over an AI coding agent deleting a user's files.
- Most Cursor and Gemini incident details come from user reports, not vendor postmortems.

## 4. Prompt injection from third-party content (malicious tutorials, web pages, images)

### Takeaway
OWASP's top-ranked LLM risk (LLM01:2025) explicitly covers indirect and multimodal injection: instructions hidden in web pages, documents or images that the model reads. Its mitigations are to segregate and label untrusted content, use least privilege, require human approval for high-risk actions, validate output deterministically, and red-team. NIST AI 600-1 (July 2024) treats prompt injection as part of its "Information Security" risk. I found no lawsuit or regulator action against an app over harm from prompt injection. The Amazon Q incident shows a supply-chain variant of the same failure.

### Cited Findings
- OWASP LLM01:2025 defines "indirect" injection as the model ingesting external content, "such as a web page or file," that contains instructions. Scenario #2 is a webpage with hidden instructions. Scenario #7 is a "multimodal injection," where a prompt hidden in an image triggers unauthorized actions. Mitigations: constrain behavior in the system prompt; validate outputs with deterministic code; filter inputs and outputs; least privilege; "require human approval" for high-risk actions; "segregate external content"; adversarial testing — [OWASP LLM01:2025](https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm01-prompt-injection/), accessed 2026-11-21.
- NIST AI 600-1 (Generative AI Profile, a companion to the AI RMF), finalized July 2024, lists 12 generative-AI risks and over 200 suggested actions — [NIST publication page, July 2024](https://www.nist.gov/publications/artificial-intelligence-risk-management-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence), accessed 2026-11-21. Its information-security risk includes prompt injection, and NIST distinguishes direct from indirect injection — [IBM summary (secondary)](https://www.ibm.com/think/insights/ai-prompt-injection-nist-report), accessed 2026-11-21. A secondary guide cites action MS-2.7-007 (red-team for prompt injection resilience) — [Anaconda guide (secondary)](https://www.anaconda.com/blog/nist-ai-600-1-generative-ai-profile-guide), accessed 2026-11-21. I did not verify the action ID in the NIST PDF.
- Anthropic's Claude Code guidance on untrusted content: avoid piping it directly to the model; review commands; use VMs; web pages go through a separate model call that summarizes them instead of being handed in raw — [Claude Code docs, Security](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/security), accessed 2026-11-21.
- Amazon Q: a malicious instruction entered a widely distributed AI coding tool through an unreviewed contribution. AWS's response was to revoke credentials and change its contribution guidelines — [AWS-2025-015, July 23, 2025](https://aws.amazon.com/security/security-bulletins/AWS-2025-015/), accessed 2026-11-21.
- The states' notice says safeguards "degrade in long interactions" and "can be circumvented through user interactions" — [States' Notice ¶ 5, Oct. 14, 2026](https://ag.ny.gov/sites/default/files/court-filings/alaska-et-al-v-characters-technologies-inc-notice-of-interest-2026.pdf), accessed 2026-11-21.

### Inferences
- miyagi screenshots a third party's video and reads its on-screen text. That is exactly OWASP's indirect plus multimodal injection path: a creator can put instructions in a video frame, a code comment, or a description. The highest-severity chain is screenshot text, then a model-written command, then one-click execution.
- Since OWASP lists "require human approval" and "segregate external content" as standard mitigations, failing to do either would be hard to defend as reasonable once harm occurs.

### Gaps
- I found no lawsuit, FTC statement or AG action specifically about prompt-injection harm to an end user.
- I did not verify the exact NIST AI 600-1 action text on prompt injection.

## 5. Defamation by AI output and relevance to commenting on code and tutorial creators

### Takeaway
The only merits ruling, Walters v. OpenAI (Ga. Super. Ct., May 19, 2025), went for OpenAI. The court found no defamatory meaning because a reasonable reader (given the disclaimers and context) would not take the output as fact, no negligence was shown, the plaintiff could not show actual malice as a public figure, and there were no damages. But Starbuck v. Google survived a motion to dismiss in Delaware (reported July 2026), Starbuck v. Meta settled (2025), and Wolf River Electric v. Google (AI Overviews) is pending. Disclaimers help, but they do not end claims at the pleading stage.

### Cited Findings
- Walters v. OpenAI (Gwinnett Cnty. Super. Ct.), summary judgment for OpenAI on May 19, 2025. Grounds: (1) the output was not defamatory as a matter of law, since the journalist could not reasonably read it as fact given OpenAI's repeated warnings that the tool can be inaccurate and the obvious knowledge-cutoff problem; (2) there was no evidence of negligence, and the court credited OpenAI's error-reduction efforts; (3) as a public figure, the plaintiff showed no actual malice; (4) he showed no damages — [Volokh Conspiracy/Reason, May 20, 2025](https://reason.com/volokh/2025/05/20/openai-wins-libel-lawsuit-brought-by-gun-rights-activist-over-hallucinated-embezzlement-claims/), accessed 2026-11-21; [Loeb & Loeb, May 2025 (secondary)](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2025/05/walters-v-openai-llc), accessed 2026-11-21. It is a state trial-court order with limited precedential weight.
- Starbuck v. Meta settled in 2025. Starbuck became a Meta adviser on bias and hallucinations, and the terms were otherwise undisclosed (reported by WSJ) — [Quinn Emanuel client alert, 2025 (secondary)](https://www.quinnemanuel.com/the-firm/publications/client-alert-defamation-in-the-ai-era/), accessed 2026-11-21.
- Starbuck v. Google (Del. Super. Ct., filed Oct. 2025): the motion to dismiss was denied (reported July 24, 2026). The judge called it "a new frontier for defamation cases" — [Epoch Times, July 2026 (secondary)](https://www.theepochtimes.com/us/conservative-activists-ai-defamation-case-against-google-can-move-forward-judge-rules-6067935), accessed 2026-11-21; [Fox Business, Oct. 2025](https://www.foxbusiness.com/media/robby-starbuck-why-he-sued-google-outrageously-false-information-through-artificial-intelligence), accessed 2026-11-21. PENDING.
- Wolf River Electric v. Google: a Minnesota solar company alleges AI Overviews falsely said the state AG had sued it. Filed in state court in 2025 and removed to federal court; a law professor noted federal courts' receptiveness to Section 230 — [University of Minnesota Law, June 13, 2025](https://law.umn.edu/news/2025-06-13-dean-william-mcgeveran-quoted-star-tribune-about-lawsuit-regarding-ai-false), accessed 2026-11-21; [SettlementInsight (secondary, 2026)](https://settlementinsight.com/wolf-river-electric-lawsuit), accessed 2026-11-21. No merits ruling found. PENDING.

### Inferences
- For miyagi, the risk is the coach making false factual claims about a named creator, for example "this tutorial installs malware" or "this creator plagiarized." Critiquing the code ("this line has a bug") is opinion about the work and much lower risk. Walters suggests visible accuracy warnings and framing feedback as AI evaluation help with the "reasonable reader" question. Starbuck v. Google shows a case can still reach discovery.
- Private-figure creators (most YouTube coding instructors) only need to show negligence, not actual malice. That makes Walters' public-figure ground unavailable in many disputes.

### Gaps
- I found no AI defamation case involving commentary on someone's code or educational content.
- I could not verify the exact date or terms of the Starbuck v. Meta settlement from a primary source.

## 6. Section 230 and AI-generated content; how much terms of service and disclaimers protect, especially against minors

### Takeaway
As of November 2026, no court has squarely held that Section 230 protects a chatbot's own generated output. The leading 2026 decision on generative AI (Bouck v. Meta) found 230 inapplicable where the platform's AI helped create the content, and commentators largely expect 230 not to cover model output. Section 230 still protects passing along genuinely third-party content, and design claims about an app's own features fall outside it (Social Media MDL). Terms of service help in some contexts (Walters' disclaimers on defamation). But arbitration and liability waivers are weak against minors, who can disaffirm contracts (Bishop v. SZ DJI, E.D. Tex. 2025), and against parents who never agreed.

### Cited Findings
- No ruling in a chatbot-harm case on whether Section 230 covers generated output, as of Nov. 6, 2026. Garcia did not address it — [recordinglaw tracker (secondary)](https://www.recordinglaw.com/us-laws/mass-tort/ai-chatbot-lawsuit/), accessed 2026-11-21.
- Bouck v. Meta (2026): a March 24, 2026 order held that Meta could plausibly be an "information content provider" of fraudulent ad text generated with its own generative-AI ad tools, so Section 230 did not bar the claim. The case was later dismissed as preempted by securities law. In the parallel Suddeth case, 230 applied because the claims targeted algorithmic amplification — [Eric Goldman, Technology & Marketing Law Blog, June 2026 (secondary)](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2026/06/section-230-doesnt-apply-to-generative-ai-enhancements-to-ad-copy-but-the-plaintiffs-lose-anyway-bouck-and-suddeth-v-meta.htm), accessed 2026-11-21.
- CDT's analysis of Section 230 and generative AI — [Center for Democracy & Technology (secondary)](https://cdt.org/insights/section-230-and-its-applicability-to-generative-ai-a-legal-analysis/), accessed 2026-11-21. Sen. Wyden, a 230 co-author, says a company that creates or helps create content is responsible for it — [Quartz (secondary)](https://qz.com/section-230-ai-chatbots-legal-immunity-generative-content-072826), accessed 2026-11-21.
- Social Media MDL: Section 230 barred claims aimed at publishing third-party content, but not design claims about the platforms' own conduct (parental controls, notifications, account deletion) — [DLA Piper, Jan. 11, 2024 (secondary)](https://www.dlapiper.com/insights/publications/2024/01/navigating-the-digital-dilemma-court-addresses-social-media-liability-in-adolescent-addiction), accessed 2026-11-21.
- Disclaimers helped OpenAI in Walters (the reasonable-reader analysis) — [Reason/Volokh, May 20, 2025](https://reason.com/volokh/2025/05/20/openai-wins-libel-lawsuit-brought-by-gun-rights-activist-over-hallucinated-embezzlement-claims/), accessed 2026-11-21. In Raine, OpenAI pleads terms-of-use violations as a defense, with no ruling yet — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raine_v._OpenAI), accessed 2026-11-21.
- Minors and terms of service: in Bishop v. SZ DJI Technology, No. 4:24-cv-268 (E.D. Tex., Sept. 30, 2025), the court denied a motion to compel arbitration under an app's terms of use. A 13-year-old had clicked "agree," and under Texas law "a minor's contract is voidable during minority and for a reasonable time after the minor reaches majority." He disaffirmed, and his parents had never agreed to the terms — [Memorandum Opinion and Order, Bishop v. SZ DJI, Sept. 30, 2025](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-txed-4_24-cv-00268/pdf/USCOURTS-txed-4_24-cv-00268-1.pdf), accessed 2026-11-21.
- In A.F. v. Character Techs., the parents argued C.AI's arbitration clause is unenforceable against children. The case settled with no ruling found — [MLex, 2025](https://www.mlex.com/mlex/articles/2327249/character-ai-arbitration-clause-not-enforceable-against-children-parents-argue), accessed 2026-11-21.
- The proposed AI LEAD Act would address "unconscionable liability limitations" for AI systems — [Senate Judiciary section-by-section (2025)](https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/Section-by-Section%20-%20AI%20LEAD%20Act.pdf), accessed 2026-11-21.

### Inferences
- For miyagi, grades, coaching and model-written commands are miyagi's own (co-)created content, so Section 230 is unlikely to help. Showing the creator's own unmodified text might be protected, but the moment the model rewrites it into a command, that protection probably falls away (per Bouck's "helped create" logic).
- Terms of service accepted by a 13-17-year-old are the weakest form of protection: minors can disaffirm, and parents who never agreed are not bound. A parent-consent step that has the parent accept the terms is the main way to make them reach the parent's own claims.
- Disclaimers work best as part of the product's design (warnings at the moment of risk), not only in legal documents. Walters relied on warnings users actually saw.

### Gaps
- I found no appellate ruling on Section 230 and generative output.
- I did not research California's specific rules on minors disaffirming contracts (Family Code § 6710 and related) or on enforcing exculpatory clauses against minors' personal-injury claims. A lawyer should confirm this for California and the users' home states.

## 7. Regulatory and industry standards a court might treat as the standard of care

### Takeaway
A set of public benchmarks now defines "reasonable" for AI used by teens and for AI that takes actions. ## Implications for miyagi

### Takeaway
The biggest exposure is the "Run it for me" / "Do it for me" button. It executes a model-written shell command in one click, built from content made by an untrusted third party, for users who may be 13-17. That combines the design-defect theory from Garcia, the excessive-agency and indirect-injection failures OWASP describes, and the agent-containment theory in California v. OpenAI. Second is teen-facing coach behavior and age handling, which is what every AG suit and settlement targets. Third is a contract problem: Google's Gemini API terms forbid apps likely to be used by under-18s. Most fixes below are engineering changes the founders can make themselves; items marked "lawyer" need counsel.

### Cited Findings (ranked safeguards, each tied to the case or rule behind it)
1. **Rebuild "Run it for me" as preview-then-confirm, never one-click.**
   - What to build:
     - Show the exact command with a plain-language explanation and risk label.
     - Require an explicit confirm for each command, with no "always allow" for teens.
     - Fail closed: anything not on an allow-list of safe tutorial commands (package installs inside the project, running the learner's own file) needs extra confirmation.
     - Hard-block destructive or privileged patterns: recursive delete, format/diskpart, registry edits, sudo/Run as administrator, `curl|sh`/`iwr|iex`, changes outside the project folder, and credential or SSH access.
     - Never run elevated.
     - Confine execution to a project directory, and sandbox it when feasible.
     - Snapshot or allow undo of the project folder before each run, and keep a local log of what ran.
   - Basis: [OWASP LLM06:2025](https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm062025-excessive-agency/) (avoid open-ended shell, human approval, complete mediation, least privilege); [Claude Code security docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/security) (read-only default, approval, fail-closed, deny-lists, sandbox); [Replit, The Register, July 2025](https://www.theregister.com/2025/07/22/replit_saastr_response/) (dev/prod separation, rollback); [AIID #1178 Gemini CLI](https://incidentdatabase.ai/cite/1178/); [California v. OpenAI, CalMatters, Nov. 16, 2026](https://calmatters.org/economy/technology/2026/11/california-is-suing-openai-over-hugging-face-hack/); Garcia's design-defect framing ([prompt20](https://blog.prompt20.com/posts/garcia-v-character-ai-ruling/)). All accessed 2026-11-21.
   - Lawyer: not needed to build. A lawyer should review the warning text shown at the button.
2. **Treat tutorial content (screenshots, transcripts, on-screen code, descriptions) as untrusted data.**
   - What to build:
     - Never let text from the video become an instruction to the model or a command without the learner clicking for that specific step.
     - Label untrusted content as such in prompts.
     - Have deterministic code (not the model) check each proposed command against the allow/deny rules.
     - Flag commands that do not match code visible in the tutorial.
     - Red-team with deliberately malicious tutorials, including text hidden in video frames.
   - Basis: [OWASP LLM01:2025](https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm01-prompt-injection/) (indirect and multimodal injection, segregate external content, human approval, adversarial testing); [NIST AI 600-1](https://www.nist.gov/publications/artificial-intelligence-risk-management-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence); [AWS-2025-015 Amazon Q wiper prompt](https://aws.amazon.com/security/security-bulletins/AWS-2025-015/). All accessed 2026-11-21.
   - Lawyer: no.
3. **Fix model-vendor compliance for teen users.**
   - Google's Gemini API terms require users to be 18+ and bar clients "likely to be accessed by individuals under the age of 18." Unpaid-tier prompts (screenshots of teens' screens) may be used for training and reviewed by humans.
   - Either stop sending teen sessions to Gemini, or move to Google terms that expressly allow it.
   - Never use an unpaid tier for user data.
   - For Claude, follow Anthropic's minors guidelines. The Commercial Terms make miyagi indemnify Anthropic for user-driven claims and require telling users that outputs should be independently checked.
   - Basis: [Gemini API Additional Terms, eff. Mar. 23, 2026](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/terms); [Anthropic Commercial Terms §§ D.3, K.2, L.3](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/commercial-terms); [Anthropic minors guidelines, Mar. 16, 2026](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/9307344-responsible-use-of-anthropic-s-models-guidelines-for-organizations-serving-minors). All accessed 2026-11-21.
   - Lawyer: yes, to confirm which Google terms apply and whether any permit 13-17 users.
4. **Keep the coach a coding tutor, not a companion, with a crisis protocol.**
   - What to build:
     - Scope the system prompt to the coding task.
     - No persona that claims to be human, a friend, or a licensed professional.
     - Clear "you're talking to an AI" disclosure.
     - No romantic or sexual content, and no "relationship" or "I missed you" engagement hooks.
     - Detect self-harm or crisis language, stop coaching, and show crisis resources (988 in the U.S.).
     - Publish a short safety-protocol page.
     - Keep voice off for minors (testers found voice mode bypassed C.AI's text filters).
   - Basis: Garcia's alleged defects ([LexSummary](https://lexsummary.com/garcia-v-character-technologies-ai-chatbot-wrongful-death/)); Raine ([Tech Policy Press](https://techpolicy.press/breaking-down-the-lawsuit-against-openai-over-teens-suicide)); the eight states' sycophancy and "fake friend" theory ([Al Jazeera, Oct. 15, 2026](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/15/why-are-eight-us-states-suing-openai)); the states' conditions 3, 4 and 9 and the voice-mode bypass finding ([States' Notice ¶ 7, ¶ 25](https://ag.ny.gov/sites/default/files/court-filings/alaska-et-al-v-characters-technologies-inc-notice-of-interest-2026.pdf)); [SB 243 (Skadden)](https://skadden.com/insights/publications/2025/10/new-california-companion-chatbot-law); [Anthropic minors guidelines](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/9307344-responsible-use-of-anthropic-s-models-guidelines-for-organizations-serving-minors). All accessed 2026-11-21.
   - Lawyer: needed only to decide whether SB 243 applies. Building to it is cheap either way.
5. **Age assurance and parental consent.**
   - What to build:
     - Block under-13 users.
     - For 13-17, a parental-consent step in which the parent accepts the terms.
     - A teen mode (Run button limited or off, voice off) as the default when age is uncertain.
     - Do not rely only on a typed birthdate if the product is marketed to teens.
   - Basis: Kentucky's "total lack of age verification" ([KY AG, Jan. 8, 2026](https://www.kentucky.gov/Pages/Activity-stream.aspx?n=AttorneyGeneral&prId=1857)); Oregon's unverified parental consent theory ([KLCC, Nov. 17, 2026](https://www.klcc.org/crime-law-justice/2026-11-17/oregon-attorney-general-dan-rayfield-sues-openai-chatgpt-youth-safety)); states' condition 1 ([States' Notice](https://ag.ny.gov/sites/default/files/court-filings/alaska-et-al-v-characters-technologies-inc-notice-of-interest-2026.pdf)); the Social Media MDL age-verification design claim ([DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/insights/publications/2024/01/navigating-the-digital-dilemma-court-addresses-social-media-liability-in-adolescent-addiction)); the OpenAI blueprint's default to the under-18 experience ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/building-towards-age-prediction/)). All accessed 2026-11-21.
   - Lawyer: yes (COPPA and state privacy laws, and how strong age assurance must be).
6. **Truthful marketing and in-product warnings.**
   - Do not claim "safe for kids," "can't break your computer," or accuracy the product can't guarantee.
   - Put a short warning at the Run button and on grades: the AI can be wrong, review commands, use your own project folder.
   - Basis: the consumer-protection suits by Kentucky, Florida, the eight states and Oregon all rest on misrepresenting safety ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/15/why-are-eight-us-states-suing-openai); [KLCC](https://www.klcc.org/crime-law-justice/2026-11-17/oregon-attorney-general-dan-rayfield-sues-openai-chatgpt-youth-safety)); Walters' reliance on warnings users actually saw ([Reason/Volokh, May 20, 2025](https://reason.com/volokh/2025/05/20/openai-wins-libel-lawsuit-brought-by-gun-rights-activist-over-hallucinated-embezzlement-claims/)); [Anthropic Commercial Terms § D.3](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/commercial-terms). All accessed 2026-11-21.
   - Lawyer: a quick review of site copy and warnings.
7. **Defamation hygiene for comments on tutorials.**
   - Instruct the coach to critique code and steps, never the creator's character, honesty or intent.
   - No factual claims about named people.
   - Label feedback as AI evaluation.
   - Give creators a way to report a problem.
   - Basis: [Walters (Loeb)](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2025/05/walters-v-openai-llc); [Starbuck v. Google surviving dismissal (Epoch Times)](https://www.theepochtimes.com/us/conservative-activists-ai-defamation-case-against-google-can-move-forward-judge-rules-6067935); [Wolf River (UMN)](https://law.umn.edu/news/2025-06-13-dean-william-mcgeveran-quoted-star-tribune-about-lawsuit-regarding-ai-false). All accessed 2026-11-21.
   - Lawyer: no.
8. **Terms of service that reach parents, without relying on them.**
   - Terms, disclaimers, any arbitration clause and liability limits should be accepted by the parent for teen accounts.
   - Assume a minor can void them.
   - Basis: [Bishop v. SZ DJI (E.D. Tex., Sept. 30, 2025)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-txed-4_24-cv-00268/pdf/USCOURTS-txed-4_24-cv-00268-1.pdf); [MLex on A.F. v. Character Techs.](https://www.mlex.com/mlex/articles/2327249/character-ai-arbitration-clause-not-enforceable-against-children-parents-argue); the AI LEAD Act's limits on "unconscionable" liability caps ([Senate Judiciary](https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/Section-by-Section%20-%20AI%20LEAD%20Act.pdf)). All accessed 2026-11-21.
   - Lawyer: yes.
9. **Data minimization for screenshots, and documented safety work.**
   - Minimize data: no training on users' screens, short retention, no unpaid model tiers.
   - Keep a dated record of risk decisions and red-team results (NIST-style) as evidence of reasonable care.
   - Basis: states' conditions 5 and 7 ([States' Notice](https://ag.ny.gov/sites/default/files/court-filings/alaska-et-al-v-characters-technologies-inc-notice-of-interest-2026.pdf)); [Gemini unpaid-tier terms](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/terms); [NIST AI 600-1](https://www.nist.gov/publications/artificial-intelligence-risk-management-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence); the FTC 6(b) questions on testing and monitoring ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/09/ftc-launches-inquiry-ai-chatbots-acting-companions)). All accessed 2026-11-21.
   - Lawyer: yes for the privacy policy. The record-keeping needs no lawyer.

### Inferences
- If only one thing changes before wider release, it should be item 1 combined with item 2: no command runs without the learner seeing it and confirming it, and nothing from the video can trigger a command on its own. That closes the most severe, best-documented failure mode (irreversible damage to a teen's computer) and the newest regulator theory (agent containment).
- A free product built by students is still a "product" under the Garcia framing, so being free or small is not a defense. Plaintiffs in these cases also sued individual founders (Garcia kept the C.AI founders in pending jurisdictional discovery; Kentucky named the founders).
- Forming a company and getting insurance before wider distribution is a common way to separate personal and business liability, but I found no source in this research on that point. It needs a lawyer.

### Gaps
- I found no case involving an AI tutoring or education app specifically, and none involving harm caused by executing an AI-suggested command on a user's own machine.
- I did not research entity formation, insurance, or whether minors can be personally liable as developers.
- Whether Google offers teen-permitted Gemini terms, and whether SB 243 is currently enjoined, remain open and need a lawyer.
