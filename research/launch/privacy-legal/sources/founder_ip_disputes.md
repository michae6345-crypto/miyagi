## 1. How common and costly are cofounder disputes, and what triggers them?

### Takeaway
Wasserman's data puts "people problems" behind about 65% of failures at high-potential startups. The best-known founder lawsuits (ConnectU/Facebook, about $65M; Reggie Brown/Snapchat, $157.5M) were all claims by someone who was there at the start and had no clear written deal about ownership or equity. The usual triggers are equity splits made in the first month, no vesting, unassigned IP and early departures.

### Cited Findings
- Wasserman (Stanford eCorner talk, 2012; transcript hosted 2024) said about 35% of failure reasons were functional (customers and so on) and the predominant 65% were "people problems". That category covers tensions among founders and between founders and early hires, so it is broader than cofounder conflict alone. — [Stanford STVP transcript, "Reasons for failure in high-potential startups" (talk 2012)](https://stvp.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2024/09/reasons-for-failure-in-high-potential-startups-transcript.pdf), accessed 2026-11-21; [STVP clip page](https://stvp.stanford.edu/clips/reasons-for-failure-in-high-potential-startups), accessed 2026-11-21
- The popular phrasing "65% of high-potential startups fail as a result of conflict among co-founders" is a press paraphrase (secondary). CNN Money attributed it to Wasserman's study of about 10,000 founders. — [CNN Money, "Fighting co-founders doom startups" (Feb 24, 2014)](https://money.cnn.com/2014/02/24/smallbusiness/startups-entrepreneur-cofounder), accessed 2026-11-21; [Entrepreneur (n.d.)](https://www.entrepreneur.com/leadership/harvard-business-school-professor-says-65-of-startups-fail/370367), accessed 2026-11-21. Fortune's looser version says "problems within management teams": [Fortune (Sep 15, 2015)](https://fortune.com/2015/09/15/skimlinks-ceo-why-startups-fail/), accessed 2026-11-21
- *The Founder's Dilemmas* (Princeton UP, 2012) draws on data from nearly 4,000 high-potential startups and about 10,000 founders. — [Princeton University Press listing](https://press.princeton.edu/books/ebook/9781400841936/the-founders-dilemmas-pdf), accessed 2026-11-21
- Wasserman's HBS research: 73% of founding teams settle the equity split in the venture's first month, and 33% split equally, usually teams that split early (secondary summary of his research). — [HBS Working Knowledge, "How to Sink a Startup" (n.d. in retrieval)](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/how-to-sink-a-startup), accessed 2026-11-21
- Only about 16% of ventures in his data were founded solo. — [STVP, "The Founder's Dilemmas" talk transcript](https://stvp.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2024/09/the-founders-dilemmas-entire-talk-transcript.pdf), accessed 2026-11-21
- Within the 65%, startups founded by family members, married couples and friends failed most often (secondary report of a Wasserman talk). — [Built In, "Noam Wasserman's 5 lessons" (n.d.)](https://builtin.com/articles/noam-wasserman-visits-techstars-boulder-shines-light-made-data-founding-pitfalls-can-kill-your-), accessed 2026-11-21
- Carta data (secondary; the data page returned HTTP 403, so this comes from the search result summary): in a sample of 22,352 founders on two- and three-person teams, cofounder departures cluster in years 1 to 3. A later Carta post says departures in VC-backed companies may be edging down in recent cohorts. — [Carta, "Cofounder departure rates by year" (n.d.)](https://carta.com/data/linkedin-cofounder-departure-rates-by-year/), accessed 2026-11-21; [Carta, "Cofounder breakup rates may be slightly declining" (n.d.)](https://carta.com/data/linkedin-cofounder-breakups-declining-data/), accessed 2026-11-21
- Facebook/ConnectU (Winklevoss): ConnectU's founders settled in 2008 for $20M cash plus Facebook stock then valued at $45M (about $65M total). In April 2011 the Ninth Circuit (Chief Judge Kozinski) enforced the settlement over their claim that the stock had been misvalued. Later press valued the deal at $100M to $160M+ as the stock rose. — [CNN Money (Apr 11, 2011)](https://money.cnn.com/2011/04/11/technology/winklevoss_facebook_lawsuit/), accessed 2026-11-21; [National Law Review (2011)](https://natlawreview.com/article/ninth-circuit-no-friend-to-winklevoss-twins-facebook-settlement-stands), accessed 2026-11-21; [Al Jazeera (Apr 12, 2011)](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/americas/2011/04/201141252955498966.html), accessed 2026-11-21
- Snapchat/Reggie Brown: Brown's 2013 "ousted founder" suit settled in September 2014 for $157.5M. The amount became public in Snap's February 2017 S-1. Sources disagree on the timing: most say $50M was paid in 2014 and $107.5M in 2016, while CNBC says the remainder came in 2015. Fortune reports no equity was included. — [TechCrunch (Feb 2, 2017)](https://techcrunch.com/2017/02/02/snapchat-reggie-brown/), accessed 2026-11-21; [CNBC (Feb 3, 2017)](https://www.cnbc.com/2017/02/03/snapchat-paid-third-cofounder-158-million-ipo-filing-reveals.html), accessed 2026-11-21; [Fortune (Feb 3, 2017)](https://fortune.com/2017/02/03/snapchat-snap-ipo-spiegel-murphy-brown), accessed 2026-11-21
- Standard founder vesting exists to handle departures. A founder who leaves before a one-year cliff keeps nothing, and the company can repurchase unvested shares at cost. — [Carta, "Vesting" guide (n.d.)](https://carta.com/learn/equity/stock-options/vesting/), accessed 2026-11-21; [Carta, "Founder shares" (n.d.)](https://carta.com/learn/startups/equity-management/founder-shares/), accessed 2026-11-21; [Stripe, "Founder vesting" (n.d.), secondary](https://stripe.com/resources/more/founder-vesting), accessed 2026-11-21
- Unassigned founder IP is a standard due-diligence item and a source of cap-table disputes (secondary). — [Stripe, "CIIA agreements 101" (n.d.)](https://stripe.com/resources/more/ciia-agreements-101), accessed 2026-11-21

### Inferences
- The famous cases share a pattern that also fits miyagi: an early idea or code contributor with no signed paper about who owns what, and a later valuation that makes the claim worth suing over. Even claims of doubtful merit settled for large sums because they clouded ownership before an IPO or financing.
- Two friends who split 50/50 on day one with no vesting fit the profile Wasserman flags as high-risk: an early, equal split among friends.

### Gaps
- I found no reliable study ranking founder disputes among the "most common startup lawsuits". The frequency evidence is about failure causes and departures, not lawsuit counts, so the report should not claim a lawsuit ranking.
- I found no Clerky-published statistics on founder disputes.
- The Carta departure-rate figures by year could not be read in full (HTTP 403). Only the sample size and the "years 1 to 3" clustering are confirmed, and only from the search summary.

## 2. Default ownership with no paperwork: authorship, joint works, implied licenses, a California partnership by default

### Takeaway
With no paperwork, each founder owns the copyright in the code he personally wrote (17 U.S.C. 201(a)). The whole codebase is a "joint work" only if both intended their contributions to merge into one unitary whole, and in the Ninth Circuit that also requires shared intent and control (Aalmuhammed). A joint owner can use the work and license it non-exclusively alone, but cannot grant an exclusive license alone and owes the other an accounting of profits. Transferring ownership requires a signed writing. California may also treat the pair as a general partnership "whether or not" they meant to form one.

### Cited Findings
- "Copyright in a work ... vests initially in the author or authors of the work." Authors of a joint work are "coowners of copyright in the work." — [17 U.S.C. 201(a), LII/Cornell](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/201), accessed 2026-11-21
- Work made for hire: the employer or commissioning party is treated as the author (201(b)). This needs an employment relationship or a written agreement in a statutory category, and it does not apply between two unpaid cofounders. For contractors, "work-for-hire generally doesn't apply; explicit IP assignment required" (secondary). — [17 U.S.C. 201(b), LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/201), accessed 2026-11-21; [Stripe CIIA guide (n.d.), secondary](https://stripe.com/resources/more/ciia-agreements-101), accessed 2026-11-21
- Ownership may be transferred "in whole or in part" (201(d)). A transfer "is not valid unless an instrument of conveyance ... is in writing and signed by the owner of the rights conveyed" (204(a)). — [17 U.S.C. 201(d), LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/201), accessed 2026-11-21; [17 U.S.C. 204(a), LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/204), accessed 2026-11-21
- Joint work (17 U.S.C. 101, as quoted in Aalmuhammed): prepared by two or more authors "with the intention that their contributions be merged into inseparable or interdependent parts of a unitary whole." — [Aalmuhammed v. Lee, 202 F.3d 1227 (9th Cir. Feb. 4, 2000)](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/202/202.F3d.1227.99-55224.html), accessed 2026-11-21
- Aalmuhammed test (Ninth Circuit, which covers California): each author needs "an independently copyrightable contribution", and the court looks to "objective manifestations of a shared intent to be coauthors", control or superintendence, and whether "the audience appeal of the work turns on both contributions". "Control in many cases will be the most important factor." — [Aalmuhammed v. Lee (9th Cir. 2000)](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/202/202.F3d.1227.99-55224.html), accessed 2026-11-21; [Ninth Circuit Model Civil Jury Instruction 17.9, Joint Authors](https://www.ce9.uscourts.gov/jury-instructions/civil/chapter-16/17-9-copyright-interests-joint-authors-17-u-s-c-101-201-a/), accessed 2026-11-21
- Co-owners are treated like tenants in common. Each may use or license the copyright independently, subject only to a duty to account to the other for profits (CCNV v. Reid, quoted by D. Del.; Oddo v. Ries, 743 F.2d 630 (9th Cir. 1984)). The Ninth Circuit reaffirmed the accounting duty in Corbello v. Valli. — [Corbello v. Valli, No. 12-16733 (9th Cir. Feb. 10, 2015)](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca9/12-16733/12-16733-2015-02-10.html), accessed 2026-11-21; [D. Del. opinion, No. 20-316](https://www.ded.uscourts.gov/sites/ded/files/opinions/20-316.pdf), accessed 2026-11-21
- One co-owner cannot grant an exclusive license without the other's consent. A non-exclusive license from one co-owner is a defense against the other co-owner's infringement claim (secondary summary; the Ninth Circuit case usually cited is Sybersound Records v. UAV, 2008, so verify against the opinion). — [Duke Scholarly Communications, "When is 'exclusive' really not?" (Aug 19, 2011), secondary](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/scholcomm/2011/08/19/when-is-exclusive-really-not/), accessed 2026-11-21; [DOJ Antitrust Division letter, secondary on this point](https://www.justice.gov/atr/file/802536/dl), accessed 2026-11-21
- Implied license: a non-exclusive license needs no writing and "may be granted orally, or may even be implied from conduct." The usual test: a person asks for a work, and the creator makes it and delivers it intending the requester to use it. — [Effects Associates v. Cohen, 908 F.2d 555 (9th Cir. 1990)](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F2/908/908.F2d.555.88-6455.html), accessed 2026-11-21; [BitLaw copy of opinion](https://www.bitlaw.com/source/cases/copyright/effects_associates.html), accessed 2026-11-21
- California partnership by default: "the association of two or more persons to carry on as coowners a business for profit forms a partnership, whether or not the persons intend to form a partnership." A person who receives a share of a business's profits is presumed to be a partner, with listed exceptions such as wages or loan repayments. Co-ownership or sharing gross returns does not by itself create a partnership. — [Cal. Corp. Code 16202 (last updated Jan 1, 1997), california.public.law](https://california.public.law/codes/corporations_code_section_16202), accessed 2026-11-21
- Partnership property: property acquired in the partnership's name, or bought with partnership assets, is (presumed) partnership property. Property held in a partner's own name, with no partnership indication and no partnership assets, is presumed separate "even if used for partnership purposes." — [Cal. Corp. Code 16204 (last updated Jan 1, 1997)](https://california.public.law/codes/corporations_code_section_16204), accessed 2026-11-21

### Inferences
- miyagi's split authorship cuts both ways. One founder wrote the app and VS Code extension, the other the engine and cloud server. If those are separate works, each founder alone owns his half, and either could walk away with his half and block the product, because neither half works without the other. If a court instead finds one joint work (both built toward a single product at the same hackathon under one team), both co-own everything. Then either can use or non-exclusively license the whole codebase, including to a competitor, subject only to accounting for profits, and neither can grant the exclusive rights an investor or acquirer wants.
- If the founders split today, the likeliest outcome is mutual implied non-exclusive licenses (Effects Associates logic) plus a fight over whether a partnership existed and who gets any profits. Neither side would have a clean title.
- A partnership is plausible but not certain. Building a product together to launch it commercially, with a website, downloads, a Devpost entry and prize tracks, looks like carrying on a business as co-owners for profit. Splitting any prize money would add the 16202(c)(3) profit-share presumption. Federal law still vests the copyright in the individual authors, so how state partnership-property rules interact with copyright that one partner authored is an open question for a lawyer. Code held in a founder's personal account and made without partnership assets is presumed separate under 16204(d).
- All of this is fixed by written, signed assignments to one entity. 204(a) makes the writing mandatory for a transfer, and the writing should also exclude any implied-license or partnership claim.

### Gaps
- Not fetched: Cal. Corp. Code 16401 (default equal profit shares) and the dissociation/dissolution rules (16601 et seq.), and how a minor's capacity affects partnership formation. These need a California lawyer.
- No case found applying Aalmuhammed specifically to two cofounders who wrote separate software modules of one product.

## 3. Hackathon IP: MLH, Devpost, SanD Hacks 2026, sponsor tracks

### Takeaway
None of the hackathon terms I could read transfer ownership. MLH requires entrants to be the "sole owner" of what they submit and takes promotional-use rights. Devpost says makers keep "sole and exclusive" ownership and takes a perpetual non-exclusive license to display the project and use it for promotion, unless an event's Official Rules require a transfer and an award is paid. MLH's standard rules also require that code "remain public post event to be eligible for prizes". The SanD Hacks 2026 official rules page and the sponsor-track terms could not be read and must be pulled manually.

### Cited Findings
- **MLH Contest Terms and Conditions** (last updated Aug 14, 2025; governed by New York law, New York courts). This version covers the "Major League Hacking Contest" for member events in the July 1, 2025 to June 30, 2026 season. Key terms:
  - an eligible participant or team "must be the sole owner of the Application submitted";
  - entrants warrant the Application is their own original work;
  - entrants must provide written "IP Releases" for third-party content before they can be named winners;
  - materials are submitted "on a nonconfidential and non-proprietary basis" and may be used by MLH, its agents and subsidiaries for advertising and promotion;
  - MLH (defined as "Sponsor") reserves the right to edit, publish, use, adapt or modify Applications, which the fetch read as limited to advertising and promotional use;
  - participants consent to publicity use of their name and likeness "without additional compensation";
  - the contest is open to people 18+ "or minors with parental permission".

  MLH claims no ownership. — [MLH Contest Terms, GitHub MLH/mlh-policies (Aug 14, 2025)](https://github.com/MLH/mlh-policies/blob/main/contest-terms.md), accessed 2026-11-21

- The SanD Hacks 2026 Devpost rules page returned HTTP 403, and hackathon.csesatucsd.com shows only the event name to an automated fetch. — [sand-hacks-2026.devpost.com/rules](https://sand-hacks-2026.devpost.com/rules), accessed 2026-11-21; [hackathon.csesatucsd.com](https://hackathon.csesatucsd.com/), accessed 2026-11-21

### Inferences
- Under the terms I could read, the founders own the hackathon project. Devpost and MLH hold non-exclusive promotional licenses. These are harmless to ownership, but they are perpetual: the Devpost page, video and screenshots can stay up and be used in promotion indefinitely.
- MLH's "sole owner" warranty is a reason to clean up outside contributors (Section 5). Code owned by an outside PR author arguably breaches the warranty. The minors' clause ("parental permission") and Devpost's "parents register on their behalf" rule mean the founders should confirm a parent actually gave permission or registered. If not, ask a lawyer whether that matters for any prize.
- The rule that code must "remain public post event to be eligible for prizes" limits the option of making the repo private, at least until any prize is awarded and paid. Making the repo private would cut off copying but could, in theory, endanger an MLH prize.
- The Devpost exception (ownership can move where Official Rules require a transfer and an award is paid) is the one way a sponsor prize track could take rights. Each sponsor track's terms must be checked for "assign" or "exclusive license" language before accepting any award.

### Gaps
- **Date conflict:** the hacker guide found by search refers to "Sunday, February 1", but the project context says the event was in November 2026. Either the guide belongs to a different edition or the context date is off. Not resolved.
- The MLH Contest Terms version I read covers only July 2025 to June 2026. A November 2026 event would fall under the 2026-27 season terms, which I could not find, so the founders should check the current file.
- I could not read the SanD Hacks 2026 Devpost Official Rules or any sponsor prize-track terms, so I cannot say whether any sponsor took a license or assignment. The founders should save PDFs of the rules page and each track's terms as they appeared at the event.
- No single "default Devpost official rules template" was found publicly. Devpost leaves the IP terms to each poster.

## 4. AI-written code: copyrightability, Thaler, and what AI tool terms say

### Takeaway
Purely AI-generated code has no copyright owner in the US. Thaler v. Perlmutter (D.C. Cir. Mar 18, 2025; cert denied Mar 2, 2026) requires a human author, and the Copyright Office says prompts alone do not make the user an author. Human-written code, and human selection, arrangement and modification of AI output, can still be protected, case by case. Anthropic and Cursor assign their rights in outputs "if any" to the user, and GitHub claims no ownership of Copilot suggestions. Anthropic's consumer terms and Cursor's terms require users to be 18, and the output assignment is conditioned on complying with the terms.

### Cited Findings
- Thaler v. Perlmutter, No. 23-5233 (D.C. Cir. Mar 18, 2025, Millett, J., unanimous): the Copyright Act requires that eligible work be authored "in the first instance by a human being." The court did not decide how much human involvement is enough, because Thaler disclaimed any human creative input. — [Justia opinion page (Mar 18, 2025)](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/cadc/23-5233/23-5233-2025-03-18.html), accessed 2026-11-21; [Mayer Brown (Mar 20, 2025), secondary](https://www.mayerbrown.com/insights/publications/2025/03/appellate-court-confirms-copyright-statute-limits-authorship-to-humans-foreclosing-copyright-registration-for-content-solely-generated-by-ai), accessed 2026-11-21
- The Supreme Court denied certiorari on Mar 2, 2026 (No. 25-449), leaving the D.C. Circuit ruling in place. A cert denial is not an endorsement, and the question of how much human input is enough stays open. — [SCOTUS docket 25-449](https://www.supremecourt.gov/docket/docketfiles/html/public/25-449.html), accessed 2026-11-21; [Mayer Brown (Mar 11, 2026), secondary](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/03/supreme-court-denies-review-in-ai-authorship-case), accessed 2026-11-21; [Holland & Knight (Mar 3, 2026), secondary](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/03/the-final-word-supreme-court-refuses-to-hear-case-on-ai-authorship), accessed 2026-11-21
- A prompt-based authorship test case, Allen v. Perlmutter, was reported as pending (secondary). — [Goodwin (Mar 2025), secondary](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/03/alerts-technology-dc-circuit-holds-that-ai-cannot-be-an-author), accessed 2026-11-21
- US Copyright Office, *Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 2: Copyrightability* (Jan 29, 2025):
  - generative AI outputs are protectable "only where a human author has determined sufficient expressive elements";
  - prompts are instructions conveying unprotectable ideas, so prompts alone do not make the user the author of the output;
  - human expressive inputs that stay perceptible in the output remain protected;
  - creative selection, arrangement or modification of AI output can qualify, decided case by case;
  - no change in the law is needed.

  — [USCO NewsNet 1060 (Jan 29, 2025)](https://copyright.gov/newsnet/2025/1060.html), accessed 2026-11-21; [USCO AI hub page](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/), accessed 2026-11-21; [Copyright Office blog on Part 2 (Feb 2025)](https://blogs.loc.gov/copyright/2025/02/inside-the-copyright-offices-report-copyright-and-artificial-intelligence-part-2-copyrightability/), accessed 2026-11-21
- USCO registration guidance for works containing AI-generated material: Mar 16, 2023 (88 Fed. Reg. 16,190). Part 3 (training) was released as a pre-publication version on May 9, 2025. — [USCO AI hub](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/), accessed 2026-11-21
- **Anthropic Commercial Terms** (effective Jun 17, 2025; cover API keys and offerings that reference them):
  - the customer owns its Outputs, and Anthropic "assigns to Customer its right, title and interest (if any) in and to Outputs", subject to compliance with the terms;
  - Anthropic will defend paid-use customers against third-party IP claims over authorized Outputs, with exclusions (K.1, K.3).

  — [Anthropic Commercial Terms (Jun 17, 2025)](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/commercial-terms), accessed 2026-11-21
- **Anthropic Consumer Terms** (effective Oct 8, 2025): "Subject to your compliance with our Terms, we assign to you all of our right, title, and interest—if any—in Outputs." Users must be at least 18 (Sec. 2). — [Anthropic Consumer Terms (Oct 8, 2025)](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/consumer-terms), accessed 2026-11-21
- **Cursor (Anysphere) Terms of Service** (last updated Sep 3, 2026): Anysphere "hereby assigns to you all of our right, title, and interest if any in and to any Suggestions" (5.3). Users must be "at least 18 years old or the age of majority" (Sec. 2). — [Cursor Terms of Service (Sep 3, 2026)](https://cursor.com/terms-of-service), accessed 2026-11-21
- **GitHub Copilot:** GitHub "does not claim ownership of a suggestion produced by GitHub Copilot". Users keep ownership of and responsibility for their code, including incorporated Suggestions (the product-terms point is from a secondary summary; verify against GitHub's Product Specific Terms). — [GitHub Resources, "Establishing trust in using GitHub Copilot" (n.d.)](https://resources.github.com/learn/pathways/copilot/essentials/establishing-trust-in-using-github-copilot/), accessed 2026-11-21; [ConductAtlas summary of Copilot Product Terms, secondary](https://conductatlas.com/platform/github/github-copilot-product-terms/), accessed 2026-11-21. GitHub's own minimum age is 13: [GitHub ToS (effective Apr 27, 2026)](https://docs.github.com/en/site-policy/github-terms/github-terms-of-service), accessed 2026-11-21

### Inferences
- The phrase "if any" in every tool's assignment means the tools hand over whatever rights they have, which for purely machine-generated code may be none. No company owns pure AI output, so nobody can claim it from the founders. But the founders cannot stop a competitor from copying those specific portions either, because there is no copyright to enforce.
- The protectable assets are:
  - code the founders typed or substantially rewrote;
  - their selection and arrangement of the overall architecture;
  - their own non-code expression (UI text, docs, design assets);
  - trade secrets they never published. Code in the public repo is not a trade secret.
- **Age issue:** if either founder used Anthropic's consumer plans or Cursor while under 18, he was outside those terms. Both terms condition the output assignment on compliance. In practice the tools claim no ownership of outputs and the AI-only portions likely carry no copyright anyway, so the ownership risk is small. It is still a compliance issue and a due-diligence question, and a lawyer should advise on going-forward accounts. Do not paper over it with an adult opening an account for a minor's use if the terms forbid that.
- Git history, commit authorship and design notes are the best evidence of human authorship. Keep them and do not squash or rewrite history before IP is assigned.

### Gaps
- I did not verify the exact text of the 2023 registration guidance's disclosure requirement (applicants must disclose and exclude AI-generated material that is more than de minimis). Pull it from 88 Fed. Reg. 16,190 before relying on it.
- I did not confirm which Anthropic terms govern Claude Code when used through a consumer subscription rather than an API key. The commercial terms page does not mention Claude Code.
- I found no court decision yet on the copyrightability of AI-assisted (as opposed to purely AI-generated) source code.

## 5. Outside contributors to a public repo with no LICENSE and no CLA

### Takeaway
An outside contributor keeps the copyright in any original code in his PR. GitHub's "inbound=outbound" rule licenses a contribution only under a repo's existing license, and miyagi's repo has none. The founders hold at most an uncertain implied non-exclusive license to that code. Fix it with signed written assignments (or a CLA) from each contributor, or by removing and rewriting the code. A DCO alone does not work here, because it relies on a project license that does not exist.

### Cited Findings
- With no license, "the work is under exclusive copyright by default", and nobody else can copy, distribute or modify it. Publishing on GitHub only lets others view and fork the repo there, which is not a general license. For unlicensed projects that accept contributions, choosealicense suggests exploring "a contributor agreement" with a lawyer. — [choosealicense.com, "No License" (n.d.)](https://choosealicense.com/no-permission/), accessed 2026-11-21
- GitHub ToS (effective Apr 27, 2026):
  - D.3: "You own Your Content";
  - D.5: making a repo public lets other users view and fork it;
  - D.6 (inbound=outbound): content added to a *licensed* repository is licensed under that repository's license;
  - D.7: moral rights are retained except as needed for GitHub's own D.4 rights;
  - users must be at least 13.

  — [GitHub Terms of Service (Apr 27, 2026)](https://docs.github.com/en/site-policy/github-terms/github-terms-of-service), accessed 2026-11-21
- Developer Certificate of Origin 1.1 (2004, 2006, Linux Foundation): the contributor certifies "I have the right to submit it under the open source license indicated in the file". The DCO grants no license itself and depends on the project's license. — [developercertificate.org](https://developercertificate.org/), accessed 2026-11-21
- A transfer of copyright needs a signed writing (204(a)). Non-exclusive licenses can be oral or implied from conduct. — [17 U.S.C. 204, LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/204), accessed 2026-11-21; [Effects Associates v. Cohen (9th Cir. 1990)](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F2/908/908.F2d.555.88-6455.html), accessed 2026-11-21
- A contribution must be "independently copyrightable" to make its author a joint author, so trivial edits (typo fixes, one-line config changes) are unlikely to give the contributor any copyright interest. — [Aalmuhammed v. Lee (9th Cir. 2000)](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/202/202.F3d.1227.99-55224.html), accessed 2026-11-21

### Inferences
- A merged outside PR most likely makes the contributor the copyright owner of his original lines. The founders get an implied non-exclusive license to use them in miyagi, because the PR was submitted for inclusion, but the contributor can reuse the same code anywhere. The founders cannot grant an exclusive license or assign clean title to an LLC, and an implied license's scope (commercial use? sublicensing? relicensing?) is uncertain.
- Because Aalmuhammed requires shared intent and control, outside contributors are very unlikely to be joint authors of the whole app. Their claim is limited to their own contributions.
- **Fix order:**
  1. Inventory every non-founder commit with `git log` and `git shortlog -sne`, and grade each as trivial or substantive.
  2. For substantive ones, get a short signed copyright assignment to the LLC, or at minimum a broad CLA with an express relicensing right.
  3. If a contributor will not sign or cannot be reached, revert the contribution and have a founder rewrite it independently, without copying.
  4. Going forward, require a CLA bot sign-off before merging (a DCO only helps once a project license exists), or stop accepting outside PRs.
  5. A contributor under 18 can disaffirm the paper (Section 6), so a parent should co-sign.

### Gaps
- The Apache ICLA page could not be fetched (connection refused), so I have no primary source on standard CLA grant language. Check apache.org/licenses directly or use a lawyer's form.
- I did not verify whether outside PRs to the miyagi repo actually exist. The context says only that they "may have" been opened.

## 6. Documents that prevent disputes, and how they work when the founders are minors

### Takeaway
The standard kit is a founder equity agreement with vesting (four years, one-year cliff, monthly vesting, repurchase of unvested equity on departure), a technology or IP assignment of all pre-formation work to the entity, and an ongoing confidentiality and invention-assignment agreement. For founders under 18, each contract is voidable under Cal. Fam. Code 6710 until a reasonable time after 18. California's court-approval procedure (6750 et seq.) is built for entertainment and sports contracts and does not clearly cover software. The practical plan is therefore parent co-signature and parent management through the LLC, adult custodianship of the minors' interests, and written ratification at 18. A California lawyer should draft these.

### Cited Findings
- Customary founder vesting (Clerky, last updated Mar 6, 2024): monthly over four years with a one-year cliff, often with double-trigger acceleration. Founders may get credit for prior full-time work: for example, 12 months of prior work leaves 25% vested and 75% vesting over 36 months. On departure the company can repurchase unvested shares at the original price. Clerky warns that terms differ by product. — [Clerky Help, "What are customary stock vesting terms for startup founders?" (Mar 6, 2024)](https://help.clerky.com/article/1736-what-are-customary-stock-vesting-terms-for-startup-founders), accessed 2026-11-21
- Carta: four years monthly with a one-year cliff is the typical schedule, and a founder who leaves before the cliff keeps nothing. Carta's data team has argued founder vesting should run six to eight years. — [Carta vesting guide (n.d.)](https://carta.com/learn/equity/stock-options/vesting/), accessed 2026-11-21; [Carta, "Founder vesting should start at 6 years" (n.d.)](https://carta.com/data/linkedin-founder-vesting-should-be-6-years/), accessed 2026-11-21
- IP assignment structure (secondary): the founder stock purchase agreement, or a separate Technology Assignment Agreement, assigns IP created before incorporation, and the CIIAA/PIIA covers IP created after. These should be signed and dated at or before formation. — [Stripe, "CIIA agreements 101" (n.d.), secondary](https://stripe.com/resources/more/ciia-agreements-101), accessed 2026-11-21; [Cooley GO glossary, "Inventions assignment agreement" (n.d.), secondary](https://www.cooleygo.com/glossary/inventions-assignment-agreement/), accessed 2026-11-21; [Clerky startup products page (n.d.)](https://www.clerky.com/hiring), accessed 2026-11-21
- Cal. Fam. Code 6710 (last updated Jan 1, 1994): except as a statute provides otherwise, a minor's contract may be disaffirmed before majority or within a reasonable time afterward. If the minor dies in that period, heirs or a personal representative may disaffirm. — [Cal. Fam. Code 6710, california.public.law](https://california.public.law/codes/family_code_section_6710), accessed 2026-11-21; [Justia, Fam. Code 6710-6713 (2010 version)](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/2010/fam/6710-6713.html), accessed 2026-11-21
- Disaffirmance generally must cover the whole contract, and failing to disaffirm within a reasonable time after 18 can amount to ratification, including by conduct (secondary). Ratification by conduct after 18 is litigated, and outcomes depend on the facts: in Coughenour v. Del Taco (Cal. Ct. App. 2020) the employer argued four months of work after 18 ratified the agreement. — [UpCounsel, "Contracts made by minors" (n.d.), secondary](https://upcounsel.com/contracts-made-by-minors-are-void), accessed 2026-11-21; [Coughenour v. Del Taco (Cal. Ct. App. 2020)](https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2020/e072772.html), accessed 2026-11-21
- Court approval (Fam. Code 6750, updated Jan 1, 2025) covers contracts for "artistic or creative services" (a non-exhaustive list including "writer", "designer" and "content creator"). It also covers contracts disposing of literary, musical or dramatic properties, or likeness rights, for entertainment uses, and sports contracts. Software development and general IP licensing are not named. — [Cal. Fam. Code 6750, california.public.law](https://california.public.law/codes/family_code_section_6750), accessed 2026-11-21
- Minors and LLCs (secondary, mostly not California-specific):
  - a common structure has an adult hold the minor's interest as custodian under the state's Uniform Transfers to Minors Act (in California, Prob. Code 3900 et seq.);
  - under it the minor has beneficial ownership while the custodian holds legal title with trustee-like duties;
  - a manager-managed LLC with a parent as manager is common;
  - a minor cannot act as an agent to bind the LLC and may be able to disaffirm an operating agreement.

  — [terms.law, "Minors as LLC members" (Apr 24, 2025), secondary](https://terms.law/2025/04/24/minors-as-llc-members/), accessed 2026-11-21; [CA Board of Equalization annotation on CUTMA](https://boe.ca.gov/lawguides/property/current/ptlg/annt/625-0055.html), accessed 2026-11-21; [Justia, Cal. Prob. Code 3900-3925 (2007 version)](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/2007/prob/3900-3925.html), accessed 2026-11-21; [Kiplinger, secondary](https://www.kiplinger.com/personal-finance/how-to-save-money/family-savings/601212/gifts-to-minors-llcs-can-protect-them-from), accessed 2026-11-21

### Inferences
- An assignment signed only by a 16- or 17-year-old is real but fragile: he can undo it until shortly after 18. The protection comes from four layers:
  - parents signing as guarantors and consenting parties;
  - the LLC acting through an adult manager;
  - a fairness record (each founder gets equity in return, which makes disaffirmance less attractive, since disaffirmance is generally all-or-nothing and would mean giving up the equity);
  - a signed ratification at 18.
- Because 6750 court approval likely does not reach software, do not count on it. Ask a lawyer whether a petition is worth trying. The "writer/designer/content creator" wording is non-exhaustive but aimed at entertainment.
- Corporate templates (Clerky, Cooley GO Docs, YC/Orrick forms) assume a Delaware C-corp, adult founders and stock. For a California LLC with minor members, the same ideas carry over: units instead of shares, an LLC repurchase right instead of a stock repurchase option, and a "contribution and assignment agreement" instead of a stock purchase agreement. They need adaptation by a lawyer, not a form fill.

### Gaps
- No source found on whether a parent's co-signature removes a minor's 6710 right to disaffirm. Treat it as an open question for counsel.
- YC and Orrick founder-document templates were not retrieved in this pass. I found no template written specifically for minor founders.
- Tax treatment (for example, profits interests or an 83(b)-type election for LLC units, and CUTMA tax effects) was not researched.
- California LLC Act provisions on minor members (Corp. Code 17701 et seq.) were not fetched.

## 7. Choosing a license for the public repo on purpose

### Takeaway
Having no LICENSE already means "all rights reserved": others may view and fork the repo on GitHub but may not legally copy, modify or ship the code. Anyone can still read it, and AI-only portions may not be protected at all. A permissive license (MIT or Apache) invites copying. Source-available licenses such as FSL or BSL let people read and use the code while barring competing commercial use for a period. MLH's rule that code stay public for prize eligibility limits the private option.

### Cited Findings
- No license means exclusive copyright by default. A public GitHub repo grants only view and fork rights on GitHub. — [choosealicense.com "No License" (n.d.)](https://choosealicense.com/no-permission/), accessed 2026-11-21; [GitHub ToS D.5 (Apr 27, 2026)](https://docs.github.com/en/site-policy/github-terms/github-terms-of-service), accessed 2026-11-21
- Functional Source License (Sentry, 2023): a "Fair Source" license that converts to Apache 2.0 or MIT after two years and bars competing uses until then. — [fsl.software (n.d.)](https://fsl.software/), accessed 2026-11-21; [Sentry blog introducing FSL (Nov 2023)](https://blog.sentry.io/introducing-the-functional-source-license-freedom-without-free-riding/), accessed 2026-11-21; [TechCrunch (Nov 20, 2023), secondary](https://techcrunch.com/2023/11/20/with-functional-source-license-sentry-wants-to-grant-developers-freedom-without-harmful-free-riding/amp), accessed 2026-11-21
- Business Source License: source-available, allows non-production use, restricts production use unless the licensor grants it, and converts to an open-source license on a licensor-set change date. — [Wikipedia, "Business Source License"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_Source_License), accessed 2026-11-21; [FOSSA guide to source-available licenses, secondary](https://fossa.com/blog/comprehensive-guide-source-available-software-licenses/), accessed 2026-11-21; [Fair Source license list](https://fair.io/licenses/), accessed 2026-11-21
- MLH standard rules: code "must remain public post event to be eligible for prizes." — [MLH standard hackathon rules (n.d.)](https://github.com/MLH/mlh-policies/blob/main/standard-hackathon-rules.md), accessed 2026-11-21

### Inferences
- For miyagi, all-rights-reserved is the strongest legal position, but it only works once the code is owned by one entity. Today a "Copyright miyagi LLC" notice would be inaccurate, because the code is owned by the two founders and possibly by contributors. Order of operations: assign the IP to the LLC, then add a LICENSE or COPYRIGHT file reading "Copyright (c) 2026 [LLC name]. All rights reserved." plus a short no-license notice and a CONTRIBUTING.md requiring a CLA.
- Moving the engine and cloud server, the parts most valuable to a competitor, into a private repo protects them as trade secrets going forward. Code already published stays public, since forks and caches may exist. Check first whether this breaks MLH prize eligibility.
- An open-source license is irreversible for code already released under it. Do not add MIT or Apache casually. If openness helps distribution, FSL is the closest fit for blocking competitors.

### Gaps
- I found no case law on enforcing a no-license public repo against a commercial copier. The legal conclusion is clear, but how practical enforcement would be was not researched.

## Implications for miyagi

### Takeaway
Right now nobody holds clean title to miyagi. Each founder owns his own code (or co-owns all of it as a joint work), any outside contributor owns his lines, the AI-only parts may be unowned, a California partnership may exist by default, and every signature is voidable until about 18. Fix it in this order: freeze and inventory, sign an interim founders' agreement, form the LLC, assign the IP, clean up contributors, add a license notice, then ratify at 18. The founders below are Founder A (app and VS Code extension) and Founder B (engine and cloud server).

### Documents and steps, in order (inferences built on the cited findings above)
1. **Freeze and inventory (no lawyer needed, do it now).**
   - Stop merging outside PRs.
   - Export the contributor list (`git shortlog -sne` across all branches) and mark each non-founder commit as trivial or substantive (Section 5).
   - Record which accounts own the GitHub repo, domains, Devpost project, store listings and cloud accounts.
   - Save PDFs of the MLH Contest Terms and Hackathon Rules in force for the event, the SanD Hacks 2026 Devpost Official Rules, and every sponsor-track rule the team entered (Section 3). Then resolve the February-vs-November date question.
   - Keep the full git history. It is the evidence of human authorship (Section 4).
2. **Interim Founders' Agreement, also called a pre-formation memorandum (sign now; a lawyer review is strongly advised but this can come first).** Signed by both founders and a parent of each. It should say:
   - the whole codebase, including both halves, is intended as one product for one future company;
   - neither founder will license, sell, publish under an open license or reuse the code without the other's written consent;
   - each will assign all miyagi IP to the company when it is formed;
   - the equity split, and that it vests;
   - what happens if one founder leaves before formation (for example, he gets back his own modules with no rights in the company, or a buyout);
   - confidentiality;
   - no partnership profit claims outside the company;
   - a promise to ratify at 18.

   Even though it is voidable, it is strong "objective manifestation" evidence (Aalmuhammed) and defeats a later claim of contrary intent.
3. **Form the California LLC (lawyer needed).** File Articles of Organization and adopt a manager-managed structure with a parent as manager. Decide whether each founder holds units directly or through a CUTMA custodian (Prob. Code 3900 et seq.). **Operating Agreement**, drafted by a lawyer, must cover:
   - units and the split;
   - vesting, for example four years with a one-year cliff, monthly, with credit for hackathon and pre-formation work, or a shorter schedule adapted to students;
   - the LLC's right to repurchase unvested units at cost when a founder leaves, and a defined "leaving" (for example, stops contributing for X months);
   - decision rules: unanimous consent for selling the company, licensing the core IP, admitting members and taking money; deadlock resolution;
   - transfer restrictions and right of first refusal;
   - each founder's IP assignment as a condition of getting units;
   - confidentiality and non-solicitation of the codebase;
   - dispute resolution in California;
   - parent consents and joinders;
   - a ratification-at-18 covenant.
4. **Technology / IP Contribution and Assignment Agreement, one per founder (lawyer needed).** Each founder assigns all right, title and interest in all miyagi code, designs, docs, names and logos, domains and accounts created before formation, including all hackathon work, to the LLC in exchange for his units. It must be a signed writing (17 U.S.C. 204(a)). It should include:
   - a waiver of any implied license or partnership claim;
   - a further-assurances clause and a power of attorney so the LLC can perfect the title;
   - a parent co-signature and guarantee;
   - a confirmatory re-assignment to be signed at 18.

   Transfer the GitHub repo to an LLC-controlled organization at the same time.
5. **Confidential Information and Invention Assignment Agreement (CIIAA) from each founder (lawyer needed, standard form adapted).** Covers future work for the LLC. Requires the California Labor Code 2870 carve-out notice if either founder is ever paid as an employee. This was not researched here; flag it to counsel.
6. **Contributor cleanup (lawyer form, then founders run it).** For each substantive outside contribution, get a short signed copyright assignment to the LLC, with a parent co-signature if the contributor is a minor. If you cannot get it, revert the contribution and rewrite it cleanly. Then add a CLA requirement, such as a CLA bot, to CONTRIBUTING.md, or stop taking outside PRs. A DCO alone does not work while there is no project license (Section 5).
7. **License notice (no lawyer needed once step 4 is done).** Add a LICENSE or COPYRIGHT file: "Copyright (c) 2026 [LLC]. All rights reserved." Do not add MIT or Apache. Decide later whether to make the engine and server private (check the MLH prize "remain public" rule first) or to publish under FSL (Section 7).
8. **AI-written code (no lawyer needed for record-keeping; lawyer for accounts).**
   - Do not count on copyright in AI-only code. Protect value by keeping core engine logic private going forward, and treat the architecture, human-written code, UI text and brand as the protectable assets.
   - Keep commit history and design notes showing human direction and edits.
   - Ask counsel how to bring AI tool accounts into compliance, since Anthropic's consumer terms and Cursor require users to be 18 and condition the output assignment on compliance. Do not have an adult open an account for a minor's use if a tool's terms forbid that.
   - If the LLC ever registers a copyright, disclose and exclude AI-generated material per the USCO guidance.
9. **Hackathon terms (no lawyer needed unless a track assigns rights).**
   - Confirm that the MLH and Devpost terms leave ownership with the team. On the versions read, they do; only non-exclusive promotional licenses were granted.
   - Confirm a parent gave permission or registered, as MLH and Devpost require for minors.
   - Check each sponsor track's terms for "assign", "exclusive" or "transfer upon award" language before accepting any prize (the Devpost 7B(iii) exception).
   - Make sure the LLC's assignment takes effect subject to those existing non-exclusive promotional licenses.
10. **At each founder's 18th birthday (lawyer prepares a short form).** Sign a ratification of the Operating Agreement and the IP assignment, and a confirmatory assignment, so the 6710 disaffirmance window closes on the record rather than by argument over "reasonable time".

### Which items need a lawyer
- **Must have a lawyer:** the Operating Agreement; the IP Contribution and Assignment Agreements; the minors' capacity strategy (parent co-signature, custodianship, whether 6750 court approval is worth a petition); partnership and tax questions; the AI tool age-compliance question; any sponsor-track transfer clause.
- **Lawyer review advised:** the interim Founders' Agreement, the contributor assignment form and CLA, and the ratification form.
- **Founders can do alone:** the freeze and inventory, saving the hackathon terms, the git-history preservation, the LICENSE notice text after assignment, and running the contributor outreach. Outreach means sending a form the lawyer approves, and only once the team decides to send it.
