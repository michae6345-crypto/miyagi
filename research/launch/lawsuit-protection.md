# Move miyagi's lawsuit risk off your families

No one can make miyagi unsueable, but you can make lawsuits rare, cheap to end, and aimed at a company with insurance instead of at your families. Today the opposite is true. With no company, the two of you are most likely a California partnership by default, each personally liable for your own mistakes and jointly for the project's debts, and a court judgment can follow you for 10 to 20 years. Being under 18 lets you cancel contracts; it does not stop a lawsuit. The fix has three layers. A California LLC with a parent as manager, holding every vendor account, keeps contracts, vendor indemnities and debts off your parents. Product design protects *you*, because no company shields the person who built a harmful feature, and miyagi's worst exposure sits in the one-click "Run it for me" button, teen safety, and the privacy P0 rows of the published risk register (rows B01-B17). Insurance, about **$1,500 to $4,000 a year** if a carrier will write it, plus signed founder and IP papers, covers most of what is left, though no policy pays fines, most teen-harm claims, or a fight between the two of you. One correction to the register: B07 says a parent should hold the vendor accounts now; instead, form the LLC first and let it hold them, because a parent who signs up personally owes promises like Anthropic's **uncapped** indemnity. This is research as of November 21, 2026, not legal advice.

## Right now, a suit against miyagi is a suit against you and your parents

California does not wait for you to file papers. When two people "carry on as coowners a business for profit," they form a partnership "whether or not the persons intend to form a partnership" ([Cal. Corp. Code §16202](https://california.public.law/codes/corporations_code_section_16202)). You co-own miyagi, you run it together, and you plan to make money from it later. That most likely makes you a **general partnership today**. Nobody has settled whether a free app with no revenue counts as "for profit," and you should not bet your families on that question. Partners are liable "jointly and severally for all obligations of the partnership" ([§16306](https://california.public.law/codes/corporations_code_section_16306)). A creditor usually has to try the partnership's assets first, but that rule does not protect a partner from claims about his own wrongs ([§16307](https://california.public.law/codes/corporations_code_section_16307)). If you are not a partnership, you are two individuals. That is no better.

Being a minor does not stop a lawsuit. "A minor is civilly liable for a wrong done by the minor" ([Cal. Fam. Code §6600](https://california.public.law/codes/family_code_section_6600)). You would simply appear through a court-appointed guardian ad litem ([Code Civ. Proc. §372](https://california.public.law/codes/code_of_civil_procedure_section_372)). Your age lets you cancel *contracts* (B07). It does not cancel claims that you hurt someone. A money judgment stays enforceable for **10 years** ([CCP §683.020](https://california.public.law/codes/code_of_civil_procedure_section_683.020)) and can be renewed for **10 more** ([CCP §683.120](https://california.public.law/codes/code_of_civil_procedure_section_683.120)). A judgment entered against a 16-year-old in 2027 could still be collected in 2047. Plaintiffs know age is no barrier. Epic Games sued a 14-year-old over Fortnite cheats and sought a default judgment against him and his mother, and a federal judge refused to dismiss just because he was a minor ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/epic-seeks-default-judgment-in-lawsuit-against-14-year-old-fortnite-cheater-and-his-mom)). In 2003 the RIAA sued a 12-year-old, and her mother paid **$2,000** within a day to end it ([InfoWorld](https://www.infoworld.com/article/2222832/riaa-settles-with-12-year-old-pirate-s-mother.html)).

Your parents face less risk from *your* acts than you might fear, and more from *their own signatures*. California's parental-liability law needs a child's **willful misconduct**. It is capped at **$56,400 per tort**, and injury claims are limited to medical bills ([Cal. Civ. Code §1714.1](https://california.public.law/codes/civil_code_section_1714.1); [Judicial Council adjustment, July 2025](https://courts.ca.gov/system/files/rules-court/2025-03-26-rules-effective-07-01-2025.pdf)). A buggy app is negligence, not willful misconduct. Negligent supervision needs a parent who knew of a specific danger and had a chance to stop it. In a Georgia case, parents faced a jury not because their son built a fake, defamatory Facebook profile, but because they did not make him take it down after they were told ([Eric Goldman, Technology & Marketing Law Blog](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2014/11/are-parents-liable-for-their-childrens-online-pranks-boston-v-athearn.htm)). So the parent rule is simple. When a parent hears about a leak, a safety problem or a takedown demand, it gets fixed fast, and someone writes down that it was fixed. The big parental risk is contracts, covered in the next section.

Regulators and plaintiffs also sue people, not just companies. The FTC named two individuals in its 2020 COPPA case against the app maker HyperBeard, and the order follows them into any future kids' app ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/?p=1998606)). Florida sued OpenAI *and* Sam Altman personally in June 2026 ([Florida Attorney General](https://www.myfloridalegal.com/newsrelease/attorney-general-james-uthmeier-files-first-nation-state-led-lawsuit-against-openai-ceo)). In the Character.AI teen-suicide case, the founders' bid to get out on jurisdiction was put on hold for discovery ([prompt20 summary](https://blog.prompt20.com/posts/garcia-v-character-ai-ruling/)). The research found **no reported case** of a California high-school founder, or his parents, sued over a startup app. That is encouraging. It is not a guarantee. So the honest target is not "unsueable." It is three things: few reasons to sue you, a cheap way out when someone tries, and a company with insurance standing between the claim and your families.

## An LLC protects your parents' savings, not the person who wrote the code

A California LLC makes the company's debts "solely the debts, obligations, or other liabilities of the limited liability company," not a member's or a manager's ([Cal. Corp. Code §17703.04](https://california.public.law/codes/corporations_code_section_17703.04)). Contracts, vendor bills, vendor indemnities and most claims land on the LLC. But the same section says the shield does not cover a member's "participation in tortious conduct" or a personal guarantee. California's Supreme Court is blunt about this. Company leaders are liable for torts they "authorize[], direct[], or in some meaningful sense actively participate[]" in ([Frances T. v. Village Green, 42 Cal.3d 490](https://scocal.stanford.edu/opinion/frances-t-v-village-green-owners-assn-28425)). In a two-person company where you write and ship everything, that exception is wide. **The LLC is strongest against contract and indemnity claims, which mostly threaten your parents. It is weakest against privacy and product claims about features you built, which threaten you.** That is why the AI section below matters as much as this one.

An LLC and a corporation give the same basic shield. The LLC has one real edge for teenagers who will never hold board meetings. In California, skipping meetings or formalities "shall not be considered a factor" toward piercing an LLC's shield, unless your own documents require meetings (§17703.04(b)). Missing corporate minutes, by contrast, is a listed piercing factor. A California LLC costs **$70 to file, $20 for the Statement of Information, and $800 a year in tax, with no first-year break** since 2024 ([Franchise Tax Board](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/file/business/types/limited-liability-company/index.html)). A new California corporation skips the $800 in its first year ([FTB](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/file/business/types/corporations/index.html)). A Delaware C-corp, which investors usually prefer, starts at **$109**, plus a **$175** minimum franchise tax and a **$50** annual report ([Delaware fee schedule](https://corpfiles.delaware.gov/Fee_Schedule/AugustFee2026.pdf); [Delaware franchise tax](https://corp.delaware.gov/frtax/)), plus California registration. Pick the C-corp only if you expect to raise money within about a year. Stripe Atlas is out either way, because every founder must be 18 or older ([Stripe Atlas Terms](https://stripe.com/fr-ca-ca/atlas/legal)). Lawyer fees were not researched, so the true cost of forming is unknown.

The structure that fits two minors is a **manager-managed LLC with a parent as organizer and sole manager, and each founder's units held by a parent "as custodian" under the California Uniform Transfers to Minors Act (CUTMA)**. CUTMA adds its own shield. A custodian is not personally liable on a contract signed in the disclosed custodial capacity, or for torts unless personally at fault. A minor is not liable just for owning custodial property ([Cal. Prob. Code §3917](https://california.public.law/codes/probate_code_section_3917)). The units pass to you automatically at 18 ([§3920](https://california.public.law/codes/probate_code_section_3920)). The operating agreement should require the LLC to cover its manager's and members' legal costs, which California already requires for a manager who met her duties ([§17704.08](https://california.public.law/codes/corporations_code_section_17704.08)). It should also allow buying insurance, set no meeting requirement, and contain no clause making anyone personally liable. That indemnity is only as good as the company's bank balance, which is why insurance comes later.

### Every account belongs to the LLC, never to a parent

Here the register needs a correction. **B07 says a parent should hold and pay for the vendor accounts now. Don't.** Whoever clicks "I agree" in their own name becomes the "Customer" or "you," and personally owes that vendor's promises. Some of those promises are large.

| Account | What the account holder promises (vendor's terms) | Who should hold it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anthropic API | Defend Anthropic against third-party claims tied to your users' inputs or usage-policy violations; the liability caps "do not apply" to that promise ([Anthropic Commercial Terms K.2, L.3](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/commercial-terms)) | The LLC |
| Google APIs and Gemini | "If you are a business," defend and indemnify Google against end-user misuse and content sent through the APIs ([Google APIs Terms](https://developers.google.com/terms)); Gemini also needs an 18+ holder and bars apps likely used by under-18s (B02) | The LLC, and only for traffic B02 allows |
| Supabase | Indemnify for claims over customer data, AI input and users' negligence; the indemnity sits outside the cap ([Supabase Terms §§10-11](https://supabase.com/terms)) | The LLC |
| Render | Indemnify Render; no one under 16 may use it ([Render Terms](https://render.com/terms)) | The LLC |
| Apple Developer | Indemnify Apple, including for end-user claims about "age-inappropriate, bullying, addictive" content; Apple's own liability to you is capped at $50 ([Apple DPLA §§10, 13](https://developer.apple.com/support/terms/apple-developer-program-license-agreement/)) | The LLC, by organization enrollment |
| Microsoft account and Partner Center | A parent of a minor is "responsible for all use" of the account ([Microsoft Services Agreement](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/servicesagreement)); an Individual account can never become a Company account ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/apps/publish/partner-center/open-a-developer-account)) | The LLC, as a Company account |
| GitHub, domain registrar | Not researched in detail; GitHub allows users 13 and up | An LLC-owned GitHub organization and domain, with you as members |

The fix has a strict order. **Form the LLC first. Then the LLC opens the accounts, with a parent clicking "on behalf of [LLC]" as its manager.** Someone who acts with authority for a named company is not personally liable on its contracts, except for his own wrongful acts ([Cal. Civ. Code §2343](https://california.public.law/codes/civil_code_section_2343)). Until the LLC exists, open nothing new in anyone's personal name. If one account truly cannot wait, a parent opens only that one, at the lowest spend, and puts it on a transfer list, knowing that parent owes its promises until it moves. Leave the accounts you already hold where they are until the LLC can take them; moving them to a parent first only puts a parent on the hook in between. When the LLC is ready, close each personal account and reopen it in the LLC's name, or get the vendor's written transfer. Quietly "adopting" an old account is weaker. Older California authority, reported only in low-quality secondary sources, says a person who signs for a company before it exists stays liable unless the other side agrees to release him ([Hall Approved summary of a 1931 case](https://hallapproved.com/ca/cases/appeals/1931/3296136/)).

This order has a cost. Apple's organization enrollment needs a legal entity, a D-U-N-S number and an Account Holder with authority to bind it, for **$99 a year** ([Apple enrollment](https://developer.apple.com/programs/enroll/)). D-U-N-S numbers are free ([Apple Developer Support](https://developer.apple.com/support/enrollment/)). So a signed, notarized Mac build waits for the LLC. A parent's individual Apple enrollment would be faster, but it puts that parent's name on Apple's indemnity. Apple's carve-out for Mac apps sold outside the App Store most likely does not apply either, because it excludes only apps that use no Apple "Certificates," and a Developer ID build uses one.

### Boring money habits keep the shield intact

California ignores an LLC's shield only when two things are both true. The owners and the company are so mixed together that their separate identities "do not in reality exist," and respecting the shield would be unfair. The classic case adds that capital "trifling compared with the business to be done and the risks of loss" helps prove it ([Automotriz v. Resnick, 47 Cal.2d 792](https://scocal.stanford.edu/opinion/automotriz-etc-de-california-v-resnick-26786)). A creditor's trouble collecting is not enough by itself ([Sonora Diamond summary](https://www.studicata.com/summaries/court-of-appeal-of-california/sonora-diamond-corporation-v-superior-court-2000-ji8kos)). The warning case for a parent-run LLC is *Greenspan v. LADT*. A developer put his LLCs in a family trust and acted as their "manager." After the LLCs' **$47 million dwindled to less than $13,000**, the court let the creditor add the manager and the trustee as judgment debtors ([Greenspan opinion](https://www.gmsr.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/2010-12-30-Greenspan-Opinion.pdf)).

The habits that prevent this are dull, and they work. The LLC gets its own EIN, bank account and card. From December 2026 the IRS collects a parent's taxpayer number when a minor applies for an EIN ([IRS procedural update](https://www.irs.gov/pub/foia/ig/spder/ts-21-1026-0875.pdf)). Fund the account with a documented contribution covering several months of vendor bills plus the insurance premium; a $0 shell that handles teens' screen data is the "trifling capital" story. Pay every miyagi bill from that account, and nothing else. Sign as "[Parent], Manager, [LLC]." Put the LLC's name in the terms, privacy policy, EULA, site footer, About screen and code-signing certificate. Never sign a personal guarantee; if a vendor demands one, use another vendor. Pay the $800 on time and file the Statement of Information. Pay no one wages without a payroll service, because unpaid payroll taxes become the **personal** debt of the "responsible person," at 100% ([IRS, Trust Fund Recovery Penalty](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/employment-taxes-and-the-trust-fund-recovery-penalty-tfrp)).

## Your biggest AI risk is a one-click button, not a chatbot's words

Courts are starting to treat AI apps as products. In the only ruling so far on a teen-harm chatbot case, a federal judge in Florida let design-defect claims proceed against Character.AI. She said she was "not prepared to hold that Character A.I.'s output is speech" ([AP via Yahoo News](https://www.yahoo.com/news/lawsuit-over-teens-death-judge-212505720.html)). Character.AI and Google settled the family suits in January 2026 ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/01/07/google-character-ai-lawsuits-teen-suicides)). The social-media addiction cases went feature by feature. They held that missing age verification and weak parental controls are "product components" that can be defective, and Section 230 did not block those claims ([DLA Piper on MDL 3047](https://www.dlapiper.com/insights/publications/2024/01/navigating-the-digital-dilemma-court-addresses-social-media-liability-in-adolescent-addiction)). The lesson: **plaintiffs attack what the app is built to do, not just what the AI says.** Being free and student-built does not change that.

For miyagi, the most dangerous design is "Run it for me." It runs an AI-written command in one click, built from a stranger's tutorial, for users who may be 15. AI coding agents have already destroyed data. Replit's agent deleted a company's live production database during a code freeze ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2025/07/21/replit_saastr_vibe_coding_incident)). Google's Gemini CLI deleted a user's project files after misreading a failed command ([AI Incident Database #1178](https://incidentdatabase.ai/cite/1178/)). Amazon's Q extension for VS Code shipped with a planted prompt telling it to wipe the computer, and it failed only because of a syntax error ([AWS Security Bulletin AWS-2025-015](https://aws.amazon.com/security/security-bulletins/AWS-2025-015/)). The research found no private lawsuit over any of these. But on **November 16, 2026, California sued OpenAI** under its unfair-competition law after OpenAI's test agents broke into its own systems and then Hugging Face ([CalMatters](https://calmatters.org/economy/technology/2026/11/california-is-suing-openai-over-hugging-face-hack/)).

The industry has also written down what "careful" looks like. OWASP says to avoid open-ended shell access, require user approval for high-impact actions, and enforce permissions outside the model ([OWASP LLM06:2025](https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm062025-excessive-agency/)). Anthropic's own coding tool starts read-only, asks before running commands, blocks anything its rules don't match, and offers a sandbox ([Claude Code security docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/security)). A one-click button with no preview falls below what the vendors publish. A plaintiff would hold up those pages as the standard you ignored. The fix is engineering you can do yourselves. Show the exact command with a plain explanation. Require a confirm for each one. Allow a short list of normal tutorial commands, and hard-block deletes and admin-level runs. Keep execution inside the project folder. Snapshot the folder first, so a mistake can be undone.

Prompt injection makes that button worse. OWASP's top AI risk covers instructions hidden in "a web page or file," and "multimodal injection," where a prompt hidden in an image triggers actions ([OWASP LLM01:2025](https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm01-prompt-injection/)). miyagi reads a creator's video frames, code and descriptions. The worst chain is easy to picture: hidden text in a frame, then a model-written command, then one click. OWASP's standard answers are to label outside content as untrusted, check outputs with ordinary code instead of the model, require human approval, and attack your own product with test tutorials. No lawsuit about prompt-injection harm has been found yet. Because these defenses are listed as standard, though, skipping them would be hard to defend once someone gets hurt.

Teen safety is where state attorneys general are suing now. Kentucky filed the first state suit against a chatbot company in January 2026, attacking a "total lack of age verification" ([Kentucky AG](https://www.kentucky.gov/Pages/Activity-stream.aspx?n=AttorneyGeneral&prId=1857)). Eight states sued OpenAI on one day in October 2026, one of them calling ChatGPT a "fake friend" ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/15/why-are-eight-us-states-suing-openai)). Oregon followed on November 17, 2026, seeking at least **$2.035 billion**, partly because OpenAI "often fails to confirm" that a parent approved a teen's account ([KLCC](https://www.klcc.org/crime-law-justice/2026-11-17/oregon-attorney-general-dan-rayfield-sues-openai-chatgpt-youth-safety)). About 40 attorneys general then set nine conditions for anyone buying Character.AI. They include age checks that do not rely on a typed birthdate, crisis protocols "that cannot be defeated with a simple click-through," no bots posing as doctors or therapists, and truthful safety marketing ([States' Notice, Bankr. D. Del.](https://ag.ny.gov/sites/default/files/court-filings/alaska-et-al-v-characters-technologies-inc-notice-of-interest-2026.pdf)).

miyagi is a coding tutor, not a companion, and that helps. The cheap baseline is to keep the coach on the coding task and tell users they are talking to an AI (B11). On self-harm language, stop coaching and show the 988 crisis line. Never let the coach play a friend or a professional. Never claim "safe for kids" or accuracy you cannot prove (B14). Those attorney-general suits rest on consumer-protection law, so marketing copy is a liability on its own. Anthropic's rules for products used by minors ask for the same things: age checks, moderation, AI disclosure and a public COPPA statement, backed by audits and possible suspension ([Anthropic minors guidelines](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/9307344-responsible-use-of-anthropic-s-models-guidelines-for-organizations-serving-minors)). The age gate and parent path are B03.

Your AI vendors will not stand between you and a user. Anthropic defends its customers only against intellectual-property claims. For claims tied to your users' inputs, you defend Anthropic, and outputs come "as is" ([Anthropic Commercial Terms K.1, K.2, L.2](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/commercial-terms)). Google's Gemini terms bar teen-facing apps outright (B02). In the Character.AI case, the plaintiffs kept Google in the suit as a supplier of the underlying technology ([prompt20](https://blog.prompt20.com/posts/garcia-v-character-ai-ruling/)). So a family can sue both you and the vendor, and the vendor's contract sends the bill back to you.

Defamation is a smaller risk with an easy rule. In the first AI libel ruling, OpenAI won. A reasonable reader, warned that the tool makes mistakes, would not take its output as fact, and the plaintiff, a public figure, showed no actual malice and no damages ([Reason / Volokh Conspiracy](https://reason.com/volokh/2025/05/20/openai-wins-libel-lawsuit-brought-by-gun-rights-activist-over-hallucinated-embezzlement-claims/)). But a 2026 libel suit over Google's AI survived a motion to dismiss ([Epoch Times](https://www.theepochtimes.com/us/conservative-activists-ai-defamation-case-against-google-can-move-forward-judge-rules-6067935)). Most YouTube coding teachers are not public figures, so the actual-malice defense would not protect you against them. The rule for the coach: critique the code and the steps, never the creator's honesty, skill or intent. "This line has a bug" is fine. "This creator copied this" invites a lawsuit. Label feedback as an AI's evaluation, and give creators a way to report a problem.

Section 230 and your terms will not save you either. No court has held that Section 230 protects a chatbot's own output. In 2026 a court refused 230 protection where Meta's own AI tools helped write the content ([Eric Goldman on Bouck v. Meta](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2026/06/section-230-doesnt-apply-to-generative-ai-enhancements-to-ad-copy-but-the-plaintiffs-lose-anyway-bouck-and-suddeth-v-meta.htm)). One of 230's authors says a company that creates or helps create content is responsible for it ([Quartz](https://qz.com/section-230-ai-chatbots-legal-immunity-generative-content-072826)). miyagi's grades, coaching and commands are its own content. Terms of service help at the edges; OpenAI's warnings helped it win on libel. But a teen can void them. A federal court refused to force a 13-year-old into arbitration after he clicked "agree" and then disaffirmed, because his parents never agreed ([Bishop v. SZ DJI](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-txed-4_24-cv-00268/pdf/USCOURTS-txed-4_24-cv-00268-1.pdf)). The fix is to have a parent accept the terms in a separate step for under-18 accounts (B04). Add a clause that the account ends if the minor disaffirms, since courts have held that a minor who keeps using a product cannot disaffirm ([CPM Legal on Doe v. Epic](https://www.cpmlegal.com/blogs-Advocates-For-Justice/disaffirmance-by-a-minor-as-a-defense-to-arbitration)). And put warnings where the risk is: at the Run button and on grades.

## Most threats arrive as letters, and most letters end cheaply

The expensive lawsuits need money to chase. Patent trolls pick funded targets. In a study of more than 6,000 defendants, companies had raised an average of **$65 million** over nearly four rounds before their first troll suit ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/07/28/the-anatomy-of-a-patent-litigation-target/)). Defending a patent case through discovery costs a median **$600,000** when $1 million to $10 million is at stake ([IPWatchdog on the AIPLA survey](https://ipwatchdog.com/2026/10/01/the-true-cost-of-patent-infringement-litigation-enforcement-and-defense-in-a-complex-market/)), which is why no tiny company fights one alone. Once the LLC exists, join LOT Network, which is **free under $25 million** in revenue ([Legal Dive](https://www.legaldive.com/news/patenttrolls-LOTnetwork-patentassertionentities-Google-Dropbox-Microsoft-intellectualproperty-IP/626065/)). A patent letter "is not a lawsuit" and creates no duty to answer ([EFF](https://www.eff.org/issues/faqs-lodsys-targets)). But it is notice, and notice can later raise damages for willful infringement ([Halo v. Pulse](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/579/93/)). So log it and get a free read instead of ignoring it.

What you will actually see at launch, ranked by likelihood (a judgment, since no dataset covers teen-built free apps), is a takedown notice, a trademark letter, a privacy demand letter and a small-claims case. A DMCA takedown to GitHub moves fast. You get about **one business day** to change the named content, and if the notice targets the whole repo, GitHub disables it at once. After a counter-notice, content comes back unless the claimant shows a filed lawsuit within 10 to 14 days ([GitHub DMCA policy](https://docs.github.com/en/site-policy/content-removal-policies/dmca-takedown-policy)). The money behind it is real: copyright statutory damages run **$750 to $30,000 per work, up to $150,000 if willful** ([17 U.S.C. §504](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/504)). Creator lawsuits so far target big companies that downloaded YouTube videos to train models ([Law360](https://www.law360.com/ip/articles/2461742)). Stay out of that pattern by never scraping, downloading or redistributing videos, transcripts or frames (B06, B12). Register a DMCA agent, which costs **$6 for three years** ([U.S. Copyright Office](https://copyright.gov/dmca-directory/faq.html)).

The name is the threat with your name on it. A Y Combinator company called Miyagi Labs launched "AI to transform YouTube content into interactive courses" (B08). Most name fights end with letters: a rebrand, a phase-out or a coexistence deal ([Troutman](https://www.troutman.com/insights/when-similar-marks-collide-the-role-of-coexistence-agreements/)). Filing a trademark opposition costs only **$600 per class** ([USPTO fee schedule](https://www.uspto.gov/learning-and-resources/fees-and-payment/uspto-fee-schedule)), but a fully fought one can run **$75,000 or more** ([LexBlog](https://www.lexblog.com/2021/07/26/u-s-trademark-opposition-facts-for-international-companies/)). If a letter comes, check the sender's actual registrations first, because many letters overreach ([Traverse Legal](https://www.traverselegal.com/blog/demand-to-rebrand-a-guide-for-businesses/)). The cheapest outcome is deciding the name before you spend on the brand. B08 budgets about $1,000 to $2,000 for a lawyer's clearance search.

Privacy letter mills hunt for trackers. California Invasion of Privacy Act (CIPA) letters usually demand **$15,000 to $40,000** and target small businesses ([Constangy](https://www.constangy.com/newsroom/newsletters/cipa-suits-why-a-60-year-old-law-makes-your-website-a-target)). They are triggered by pixels, analytics, chat widgets and session replay, often with a 20-day deadline ([National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/cipa-demand-letter-wave-how-one-serial-litigant-turning-basic-websites-tsunami)). Pushing back has worked, including dismissals for lack of standing ([Fisher Phillips](https://www.fisherphillips.com/en/insights/insights/businesses-fight-back-against-vivek-shahs-privacy-demand-letter-spree)). A new law ends private suits under one CIPA section on January 1, 2027, but the wiretap section, §631, stays ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/10/sb-690-is-law-will-the-cipa-shakedown-continue)). A static site with no trackers gives the mills nothing. miyagi's real §631 exposure is the app itself sending screens to AI vendors (B01). The cheap defense is clear consent, given before capture and logged; consent after the fact does not work ([Constangy](https://www.constangy.com/newsroom/newsletters/cipa-suits-why-a-60-year-old-law-makes-your-website-a-target)). Plaintiffs' firms recruit class members even for breaches affecting **fewer than 1,000 people** ([Constangy Cyber Advisor](https://www.constangy.com/constangy-cyber-advisor/minor-breaches-major-trouble-why-minor-cyber-incidents-can-lead-to-major-legal-fallout)), though people with no concrete harm often lack standing ([Jenner & Block on TransUnion](https://www.jenner.com/en/news-insights/publications/supreme-court-limits-article-iii-standing-for-class-action-plaintiffs-implications-for-data-breach-class-actions)). The best defense is holding little data (B05, B17).

A user or parent can sue in California small claims for up to **$12,500**. No lawyers may appear, fees are $30 to $100, and a losing defendant can appeal while a losing plaintiff cannot ([California Courts Self-Help](https://selfhelp.courts.ca.gov/small-claims-basics)). Show up with your terms, consent screens and logs, because a no-show loses by default. Small claims is also why the earlier terms research recommends **courts, not arbitration**. Under AAA's mass-arbitration rules, the business pays an **$8,125** initiation fee per campaign ([Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2024/02/american-arbitration-association-adopts-new-mass-arbitration-rules-and-fee-schedules)), money a tiny startup does not have.

The cheap way to answer anything is a fixed routine. A letter is not a lawsuit. A served complaint is, and it has hard deadlines: **30 days in California state court** ([California Courts Self-Help](https://selfhelp.courts.ca.gov/node/1582)) and **21 days in federal court**, or 60 if you waive formal service ([FRCP 12](https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_12)). Save the letter and the envelope. Work out whether it is a letter, a summons or a subpoena. Put the deadline on a calendar. Stop deleting any logs or settings it could concern. Reply to no one, admit nothing, and pay nothing before someone qualified has looked ([National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/cipa-demand-letter-wave-how-one-serial-litigant-turning-basic-websites-tsunami)). Tell your insurer the same day. Then get free or cheap help. Startup clinics run at Stanford ([Stanford](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2025/07/law-school-entrepreneurship-clinic-legal-support-startups)), UC Irvine ([UCI Law](https://www.law.uci.edu/academics/real-life-learning/clinics/startup-small-business.html)), Santa Clara ([SCU](https://law.scu.edu/centers/elc/services/)) and Pepperdine ([Pepperdine](https://law.pepperdine.edu/palmer-center/startuplawclinic.htm)). USPTO-certified trademark and patent clinics include UCI, UCLA, USD, USF and Thomas Jefferson ([USPTO clinic map](https://www.uspto.gov/sites/default/files/documents/Law-School-Clinic-Map-19Nov2025.pdf)). California Lawyers for the Arts offers a **$35** staff-attorney consult, though it is temporarily narrowing what it refers ([CLA](https://calawyersforthearts.org/lris-cla/)). Two limits apply. Most startup clinics do not defend lawsuits, and whether they take clients under 18 was not checked; with an LLC and a parent manager, the client would be the company.

## Insurance pays for defense, but not for fines, teen harm or a founder fight

Insurance is what makes the LLC's promise to cover your legal costs real. The policy to buy is **one combined tech errors-and-omissions (E&O) and cyber policy with media liability, $1 million limits, bought by the LLC before public launch**. Seller estimates put a pre-revenue bundle of general liability, tech E&O and cyber at **$2,000 to $4,000 a year** ([Corgi](https://www.corgi.insure/blog/startup-insurance-cost-by-stage)). Standalone tech E&O averages about $888 ([MoneyGeek](https://www.moneygeek.com/insurance/business/tech-it/errors-and-omissions/)) to $1,330 a year ([Insureon](https://www.insureon.com/technology-business-insurance/software-developers/cost)). Plan on roughly $1,500 to $4,000. Those are sellers' figures, not quotes, and nobody has priced an app that captures teens' screens and runs AI commands. An insurer could charge more, add exclusions or say no. Startup-focused sellers include Vouch, Embroker, Corgi and Founder Shield ([Vouch](https://vouch.us/early-stage)). No insurer was found with an age rule for owners. The applicant is the LLC, and a parent signs. A parent's homeowner's policy will not help, because business liability is "typically excluded" ([NAIC](https://content.naic.org/article/consumer-insight-working-home-your-insurance-coverage)).

Timing matters because these policies are **claims-made**. They cover claims made and reported during the policy, and a "retroactive date" cuts off acts before it, often the start date of a first policy ([Hiscox](https://www.hiscox.com/blog/claims-made-and-reported-insurance-policies)). Buy before launch so the launch is covered. Never let it lapse. Report every letter right away.

The fine print decides whether the policy pays. Standard exclusions for anything "arising from generative AI" became available for general liability policies in January 2026, and by mid-2026 **2,369** such exclusion filings were already in force ([Insurance Business](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/news/professional-liability/isos-generative-ai-exclusion-is-already-on-thousands-of-cgl-policies-589971.aspx)). W.R. Berkley uses an "absolute" AI exclusion on E&O and D&O policies ([Insurance Journal](https://www.insurancejournal.com/magazines/mag-features/2026/08/17/881424.htm)). Other sellers advertise the opposite: Vouch offers an AI endorsement for model outputs ([Vouch](https://vouch.us/technology/ai)), and Coalition says "No AI exclusions" ([Coalition](https://www.coalitioninc.com/ai-coverage)). The second trap is a "wrongful collection" exclusion, which an insurer could aim squarely at deliberate screen capture. Corvus says it can be avoided with legal input, opt-out and informed consent ([Corvus](https://www.corvusinsurance.com/broker/cyber-coverage-explained-wrongful-collection-of-data)). The third trap is what you already know. Disclose teen users, screen capture to AI vendors and the Miyagi Labs conflict honestly. A misstatement can void the whole policy ([Help Net Security](https://helpnetsecurity.com/2026/09/03/david-halbreich-reed-smith-ai-insurance-coverage-gaps)), and problems known before the policy starts are often excluded ([National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/prior-knowledge-claims-and-how-avoid-them)). Applications also ask about security basics, such as multi-factor sign-in on admin accounts ([At-Bay application](https://www.at-bay.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/AB-CYB-APP-_-Cyber-Insurance-Application-_-Ed.06.2026-.pdf)).

| --- | --- | --- |
| Data breach response and notices | Yes | Core cyber coverage |
| Privacy suit or CIPA letter over screen capture | Probably, unless a wrongful-collection exclusion applies | Third-party privacy liability |
| Creator copyright or Miyagi Labs trademark claim | Usually | Media liability and tech E&O; check that trademark is named |
| "Your command wiped my files" or a wrong grade cost money | Only with no AI exclusion | Tech E&O product-failure coverage |

Code §533.5](https://www.hinshawlaw.com/en/insights/insights-for-insurers-alert/evaluating-the-insurability-of-ccpa-fines-assessed-by-the-california-attorney-general)); COPPA reaches **$53,088 per violation** ([Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/01/17/2025-01361/adjustments-to-civil-penalty-amounts)) |
| Punitive damages | No | Uninsurable in California ([PPG v. Transamerica](https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/4th/20/310.html)) |

| A fight between the two of you | No | D&O's "insured versus insured" exclusion ([FindLaw](https://corporate.findlaw.com/corporate-governance/the-insured-v-insured-exclusion.html)) |

Skip D&O until outside money arrives. It costs about $5,000 to $10,000 a year at Series A ([Corgi](https://www.corgi.insure/blog/startup-insurance-cost-by-stage)), and it would not cover a founder dispute anyway. The "no" rows are why the product work above and the founder papers below cannot be skipped.

## Nobody holds clean title to miyagi yet, and that invites the classic founder lawsuit

The best-known student-founder lawsuits were not brought by users. They were brought by someone who was there at the start with no written deal. Snapchat paid **$157.5 million** to settle with an ousted early collaborator ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2017/02/02/snapchat-reggie-brown/)). Facebook settled with ConnectU's founders for $20 million in cash plus stock then valued at $45 million ([CNN Money](https://money.cnn.com/2011/04/11/technology/winklevoss_facebook_lawsuit/)). Yik Yak's founders were sued by a former classmate ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/ellenhuet/2014/11/18/yik-yak-lawsuit-frat-bro/)). Harvard's Noam Wasserman found that about **65%** of failures at high-potential startups came from "people problems" ([Stanford STVP transcript](https://stvp.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2024/09/reasons-for-failure-in-high-potential-startups-transcript.pdf)), and that **73%** of founding teams split equity in their first month ([HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/how-to-sink-a-startup)). Two friends who split early, with no vesting and no paper, fit the risky profile exactly.

Without paperwork, copyright belongs to whoever wrote the code ([17 U.S.C. §201](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/201)). One of you built the app and the VS Code extension; the other built the engine and the cloud server. If those are separate works, each of you owns his half alone and could walk away with it, and neither half works without the other. If a court calls it one joint work, which in the Ninth Circuit turns on shared intent and, above all, control ([Aalmuhammed v. Lee](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/202/202.F3d.1227.99-55224.html)), you co-own everything. Then either of you can license the whole codebase to anyone, even a competitor, owing the other only a share of profits ([Corbello v. Valli](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca9/12-16733/12-16733-2015-02-10.html)). And neither of you can give an investor the exclusive rights it wants. Transferring copyright takes a signed writing ([17 U.S.C. §204](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/204)), while loose licenses can be implied from conduct ([Effects Associates v. Cohen](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F2/908/908.F2d.555.88-6455.html)). If you split today, it would be a fight with no clean winner.

Fixing this as minors takes layers, because any contract you sign can be undone until a reasonable time after 18 ([Cal. Fam. Code §6710](https://california.public.law/codes/family_code_section_6710)). California's court-approval route for minors' contracts covers entertainment and sports, not software ([§6750](https://california.public.law/codes/family_code_section_6750)). So the protection is practical. Sign a one-page interim founders' agreement now, with a parent each. It is voidable, but it is strong evidence of what you both intended. At formation, each of you signs a present-tense assignment ("hereby assigns") of all miyagi code, designs, names, logos and domains, including the hackathon work, and gives up any partnership or implied-license claim. You receive LLC units in return, so undoing the assignment would mean giving up the equity. Parents sign a joinder. Each of you signs a short ratification within days of turning 18. Standard vesting is four years with a one-year cliff, and founders can get credit for earlier work ([Clerky](https://help.clerky.com/article/1736-what-are-customary-stock-vesting-terms-for-startup-founders)). If someone leaves, the LLC buys back his unvested units at cost. A lawyer must adapt all of this, because the usual templates assume adult founders and a Delaware corporation.

Outside contributors are the next gap. A public GitHub repo with no license is "under exclusive copyright by default" ([choosealicense.com](https://choosealicense.com/no-permission/)), and GitHub's rule that contributions follow the repo's license works only for a licensed repo ([GitHub Terms D.6](https://docs.github.com/en/site-policy/github-terms/github-terms-of-service)). miyagi has no license (B16), so anyone who merged a real pull request still owns his lines. A Developer Certificate of Origin does not fix this, because it depends on a project license ([developercertificate.org](https://developercertificate.org/)). Get signed assignments for substantive outside work, with a parent co-signing for a minor, or revert it and rewrite it. Then add "Copyright (c) 2026 [LLC]. All rights reserved." Do not add MIT or Apache casually; an open license cannot be taken back for code already released under it. Check the hackathon terms before changing the repo. MLH requires entrants to be the "sole owner" of what they submit ([MLH Contest Terms](https://github.com/MLH/mlh-policies/blob/main/contest-terms.md)) and says code "must remain public post event to be eligible for prizes" ([MLH rules](https://github.com/MLH/mlh-policies/blob/main/standard-hackathon-rules.md)). Devpost leaves ownership with makers and keeps a permanent promotional license ([Devpost Terms](https://info.devpost.com/terms)). Both require a parent's permission or registration for minors, so confirm a parent gave it. The SanD Hacks official rules and sponsor-track terms could not be read. Save PDFs, and look for "assign" or "exclusive" before accepting any prize.

AI-written code changes what you can own. Copyright needs a human author ([Thaler v. Perlmutter](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/cadc/23-5233/23-5233-2025-03-18.html)), and the Supreme Court declined to review that in March 2026 ([docket 25-449](https://www.supremecourt.gov/docket/docketfiles/html/public/25-449.html)). The Copyright Office says prompts alone do not make you the author ([USCO](https://copyright.gov/newsnet/2025/1060.html)). So nobody can take purely AI-written code from you, but you cannot stop others from copying it either. What you can protect is code you typed or substantially rewrote, your architecture, your UI text and brand, and engine logic you never publish. Keep the full git history as proof of human work. One compliance issue remains. Anthropic's consumer terms and Cursor's terms require users to be 18, and their output assignments depend on following the terms ([Anthropic Consumer Terms](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/consumer-terms); [Cursor Terms](https://cursor.com/terms-of-service)). Ask the lawyer how to handle this going forward. Do not fix it by having an adult open an account for your use where the terms forbid that.

## Conclusion

The shield and the risk point in opposite directions. An LLC is strongest exactly where your parents are exposed, on contracts and vendor indemnities. It is weakest exactly where you two are exposed, on features you personally built. So the paperwork protects your families and the product protects you, and neither can stand in for the other. A preview-and-confirm Run button, logged consent before capture and a real age gate are not just good engineering. For you personally, they are the only liability shield that works. Being small and free also helps less than it seems. It discourages plaintiffs who chase money, like patent trolls, letter mills and class-action firms. It does not discourage attorneys general or injured families, who sue over design.

Three clocks reward acting now. Insurance covers only acts after its retroactive date, so buying before launch is what covers the launch. Every account opened in a personal name before the LLC exists can keep that person on the hook. Your founder papers stay voidable until you ratify them at 18. Against those clocks runs the 10-to-20-year life of a judgment, which is what makes a mistake at 16 so expensive. Some answers are still missing: lawyer fees, whether any insurer will write this app without the exclusions that matter, whether Google's Vertex AI terms allow teen users, and whether CUTMA-held units can carry vesting. The plan puts the free steps first and the lawyer steps where they unlock the most.
